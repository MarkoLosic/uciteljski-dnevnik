## What the Top-Performing Systems Have in Common

Among the eight countries profiled on this page, Singapore, Japan, and Estonia remain the clear standouts after PISA 2025. They share several practical habits — none of them a magic formula:

1. **Clear, focused national standards** aligned with textbooks, classroom teaching, and exams. Estonia and Finland grant schools wide autonomy, but only on the back of highly educated teachers.
2. **High-status teachers:** selective admission into training, a master's degree or tightly structured preparation, and shared school-based development such as Japanese lesson study.
3. **Regular measurement of student learning:** the PSLE in Singapore, Japan's national assessment, grade 9 exit exams in Estonia, and steady participation in PISA and TIMSS. Finland, which runs no national census tests, has slid for two decades — though testing alone does not explain the full decline.
4. **Late or flexible tracking.** Estonia and Japan keep children together until age 15, and Singapore replaced rigid secondary streams with subject-level flexibility. Germany and the Netherlands sort children early and are losing ground.
5. **Equity as a deliberate goal.** In Estonia and Japan, family background has a comparatively weak hold on achievement. The OECD counts both among just seven systems worldwide combining high performance with strong equity.
6. **Disciplined classroom technology:** strong digital infrastructure paired with clear limits on personal phones during lessons — set by law in Finland and by national agreement in the Netherlands.

## What This Means for Bosnia and Herzegovina

The main takeaway is not to copy someone else's blueprint wholesale. First, Bosnia and Herzegovina needs the basics that every strong system already has in place:

- **Countrywide participation in PISA, TIMSS, and PIRLS.** Without Republika Srpska and West Herzegovina Canton, there is neither a true national average nor a reliable trend line.
- **Low-stakes external assessment in grade 3 or 4** and a mandatory exit exam at the end of grade 9, drawing on Japan and Slovenia.
- **Shared learning outcomes in mathematics, science, and literacy** across all 13 education authorities. Three language curricula can coexist, but core outcomes should match.
- **Teacher licensing, mentoring, and mandatory continuing professional development.** Without them, curriculum reforms stay on paper.
- **More weekly hours for early reading and arithmetic**, as Finland is now funding. Slovenia shows how quickly reading falls when early literacy gets stretched too thin.
- **A smarter school network:** savings from shrinking classes should go toward teaching assistants, speech therapists, and digital learning tools — while rural schools should never close without reliable student transport.
- **Ending “two schools under one roof”** and keeping personal phones out of lessons.

Blind copying would backfire: Finnish-style autonomy without national monitoring and German-style early tracking are poor fits for a fragmented system with uneven institutional capacity. For our region, Estonia is the most realistic benchmark — a small country on a modest budget with a unified nine-year school, clear standards, and practical digital tools.

## Data Notes

- PISA 2025 scores come from the OECD report (Volume I) published on September 8, 2026. Because the PISA science framework was substantially updated in 2025, comparisons with earlier science cycles require caution.
- Bosnia and Herzegovina's TIMSS 2023 result does not cover the entire country, while Estonia (TIMSS 2023 and PIRLS 2021) and Japan (PIRLS 2021) do not have fourth-grade scores from those cycles.
- Slovenia's proposed return to eight-year primary school and Japan's next Course of Study are policy proposals rather than enacted legislation.
