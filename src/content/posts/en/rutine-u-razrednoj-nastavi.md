A lot of lesson time is lost not to "bad behaviour" but to transitions: children settling in, getting out their exercise books, handing out materials or moving into groups. A routine that is learned well once gives that time back every day, all year long. This guide is written mainly for primary class teachers (grades 1–5), with short notes for subject teachers.

## Why routines, not just rules

A rule says what is expected ("We respect others while they speak"). A routine says exactly how something is done, step by step ("When I raise my hand, you raise yours, stop talking and look at me"). To a seven- or eight-year-old, an abstract rule means little if they have never practised what it looks like.

Data on how much time goes to keeping order come mostly from research with older pupils. In the international TALIS 2018 survey, which the OECD ran with lower secondary teachers (roughly grades 6–9 in Bosnia and Herzegovina), teachers estimated that about 78% of lesson time goes to actual teaching and learning, about 13% to keeping order and about 8% to administrative tasks. That is roughly eight minutes of every hour of teaching. There is no comparable figure for primary classes, but every class teacher knows that younger children are even more sensitive to unclear transitions.

In its guidance report *Improving Behaviour in Schools*, the UK's Education Endowment Foundation (EEF) lists among its six recommendations that pupils should be explicitly taught the behaviours they need for learning, alongside responding to misbehaviour, and that simple approaches should be built into everyday routines. The report also stresses that most pupils behave well in most lessons, so routines are not "a cure for difficult classes" but a way to help a good class work even better.

## Five routines worth introducing in primary classes

You don't need to introduce them all at once. Pick the two or three that "eat" the most time.

1. **Coming in and starting the day.** Children come in, hang up their coats, sit down and immediately start a short task waiting on the board (e.g. three sums, one sentence to copy, a picture to describe).
2. **Attention signal.** One signal, always the same: a raised hand, a bell, a short clapping rhythm. Children know exactly what to do when they hear it.
3. **Handing out and collecting materials.** Desk monitors, one box per row or a "chain" from the first to the last desk, instead of everyone getting up at once.
4. **Moving into pair or group work.** Who turns to whom, where the materials go, how loudly to talk (e.g. "desk voice", not "playground voice").
5. **End of the lesson or day.** Tidying the desk, writing down homework, checking the floor is clean, leaving in line.

## How a routine is learned: explain, model, practise, praise

A routine isn't "announced"; it is taught like any other skill:

- **Explain why.** In one sentence, in a child's language: "When we all go quiet quickly, we have more time for a game at the end of the lesson."
- **Model it.** The teacher acts out the routine, then two or three pupils act it out in front of the class. Younger children also benefit from seeing a wrong example ("What did I just do wrong?").
- **Practise until it's easy.** In the first days, practise the attention signal even when it isn't needed, as a game, and time it.
- **Praise specifically.** Instead of "Well done, everyone", say what went well: "You all put your pencils down in five seconds."
- **Remind before it's needed.** Before a transition, briefly recap: "In a minute we're moving into groups. What do we do first?"

### Example: an attention signal in grade 2, the first two weeks

This is a sample plan that a teacher can adapt to their own class.

**Day 1.** At the start of the first lesson, the teacher explains the signal: when they raise a hand, the children raise theirs, go quiet and look at the teacher. This is practised five times during the day as a game, counting the seconds out loud. The result is written in the corner of the board.

**Days 2–5.** The signal is used in real situations, but with a short reminder before each transition. At the end of the day the class sees its best time and tries to beat it the next day. The reward is shared and small (e.g. two extra minutes for a game or a song at the end of the day), not individual stickers.

**Days 6–10.** Reminders are gradually dropped. If the routine "falls apart" (most often after a weekend or a holiday), don't punish it; just practise it again briefly.

Teachers who like technology can use a visual timer on the interactive whiteboard or projector so children can see how long a transition takes. An ordinary sand timer or a chart drawn in chalk on the board works just as well.

### Greeting at the door: what the research says and what needs adapting

One of the simple strategies the EEF mentions is for the teacher to greet each pupil personally at the classroom door. The best-known study on this (Cook et al., 2018) was carried out in the US in ten classes of pupils aged about 11 to 13, so with older children. Teachers were randomly assigned to two groups, and in classes where pupils were greeted at the door with a short positive message and a reminder of what was coming, academic engaged time rose by about 20 percentage points, with less disruption.

In a primary class it can be adapted like this: in the morning the teacher stands at the door and greets each child by name, and the children choose how to say hello (a handshake, a high five, a wave). The reminder is short: "There's a task with three sums waiting for you on the board." Keep in mind that the study was small and done with older children, so this is not a guaranteed recipe but a sensible, low-cost practice.

## Adapting to age

- **Grades 1 and 2:** fewer routines, more practice. Pictures of the steps on the wall (e.g. three drawings for tidying the desk), a little song or rhythm as a signal. Routines are introduced gradually during September, but it's never too late to introduce one mid-year.
- **Grades 3–5:** children can suggest a routine and a signal themselves, and monitor duties rotate. They can also assess how well the routine worked during the week.
- **Subject teaching:** every teacher has their own routines, so from grade 6 children adapt to as many as ten different systems. It helps when the subject department or class teachers' council agrees on at least two shared routines, e.g. the start of the lesson and the attention signal. This is in line with the EEF's recommendation on consistency across the whole school.

## What can go wrong

- **Complex reward systems don't guarantee results.** The EEF funded a large evaluation of the Good Behaviour Game (a team game with rules and rewards) in 77 primary schools in England, with children aged 7 and 8. No evidence was found that the programme improves behaviour or reading on average, and a later analysis showed that boys who were already at risk of behaviour problems even reported more bullying by peers. The lesson isn't that rewards are bad, but that it's better to start with simple routines than with a complex points system that singles out individuals.
- **Inconsistency.** A routine that only applies when the teacher is well rested quickly fades. If it changes, tell the children and practise it again.
- **The same routine doesn't suit every child.** The EEF stresses that universal systems won't work for all pupils. A child with ADHD, developmental difficulties or a hard situation at home needs individual support, and often cooperation with the school counsellor, psychologist and parents.
- **A routine is not a goal in itself.** If the lesson is boring or the task too hard, even the best attention signal won't help for long.

## How to start tomorrow

1. **Time one transition.** Today, use a stopwatch to measure how long it takes, e.g. from "get out your exercise books" to the moment everyone is writing. That is your baseline.
2. **Choose one attention signal** and introduce it tomorrow as a game, with at least five practice rounds during the day.
3. **Prepare an entry task.** Leave a short task on the board that children do as soon as they sit down, in the same place every day.
4. **Praise specifically** at least three times a day, describing exactly what the class did well.
5. **Agree with colleagues.** At the next staff meeting, propose one shared routine for all classes of the same grade or for all primary classes.
