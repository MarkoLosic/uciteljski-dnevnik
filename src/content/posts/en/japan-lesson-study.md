Most subject teachers spend years teaching behind closed doors: a colleague sees our lesson only when the school counsellor or an inspector visits, and then the aim is to judge the teacher, not to understand how students learn. Japan has a different habit, known as *lesson study*, in which a group of teachers plans, observes and analyses a single lesson together. This article explains how the model works, what research says about it, and how a subject team in primary school (grades 6–9) can try it without a new budget.

## What lesson study is

The Japanese name is *jugyou kenkyuu*, meaning "lesson research". At its heart is the "research lesson" (*kenkyuu jugyou*): one teacher teaches it while the other team members observe. According to data from Japan's National Institute for Educational Policy Research, cited by Catherine Lewis and colleagues, the practice exists in more than 90% of Japanese schools and is almost universal in elementary and lower secondary schools – exactly the age range covered by our nine-year primary school.

The cycle usually has four steps:

1. **Study.** The team picks a topic that students struggle with and studies it: what the curriculum says, what students learned before, what comes later, and how different textbooks handle the topic. The Japanese call this part *kyouzai kenkyuu*, the study of teaching materials.
2. **Planning.** The team writes a detailed lesson plan together. It is meant not only for the teacher who teaches the lesson but also for the observers.
3. **Teaching.** One team member teaches the lesson in their own class while the others observe and take notes.
4. **Reflection.** A discussion follows right after the lesson. Based on it, the team revises the plan and often teaches the same lesson again in another class.

The key difference from a demonstration lesson: a research lesson is not there to show off a model teacher. What is observed is student learning, and the plan is shared, so any failure belongs to the whole team.

## An example from a Japanese classroom

Tad Watanabe, a mathematics educator, described a research lesson in a sixth-grade Japanese class in the journal *Educational Leadership*. The teacher wrote four fraction-division problems on the board, from a simple one (3/5 : 1/5) to harder ones (2 : 1/4), and asked students to solve them using what they already knew about division. The discussion started with 2 : 1/4, not with the easiest problem. About thirty teachers observed the lesson.

In the discussion afterwards, a colleague asked why the lesson had not started with an easier example. The teacher explained that he had considered it but judged 3/5 : 1/5 too simple to lead students to different solution methods, and he wanted them to discover the link between division and multiplication. The discussion lasted about an hour and a half.

The example is interesting for our setting for two reasons. First, dividing fractions is a typical stumbling block in sixth and seventh grade here too. Second, the discussion was not about whether the teacher was "good" but about a concrete decision: which problem opens up thinking and which one shuts it down.

Watanabe also highlights several habits of Japanese teams that transfer easily:

- **A three-column plan:** the task, anticipated student responses (both correct and incorrect), and what the teacher does in each case.
- **A view of the whole unit,** not just one lesson: where the lesson sits in relation to earlier and later grades.
- **Observation rules:** observers move around the classroom, look at notebooks and listen to students talking, but they do not help students, even when they struggle. Their job is to record how students think.

## What the research says

The strongest evidence for the model comes from the United States. In 2017, Catherine Lewis and Rebecca Perry published a randomised controlled trial in which 39 teams of elementary school teachers were randomly assigned to three groups. Teams that did lesson study with a prepared set of fraction resources (tasks, examples of student work, videos of Japanese lessons, research) made statistically significant gains both in students' knowledge of fractions and in the teachers' own professional knowledge, compared with the control groups. The authors conclude that the combination of teacher leadership and high-quality professional materials is what produces results.

A second large trial shows the other side. The British Education Endowment Foundation (EEF) tested a lesson study–based programme in 181 primary schools with about 12,700 students. The programme involved teachers of 8- to 10-year-olds, and outcomes were measured at the end of Year 6 in English primary school. No effect on maths or reading attainment was found. Teachers nevertheless rated the programme as useful professional development and reported concrete changes in their practice. The evaluators note that some control schools already had similar practices, such as peer observation, so the effect may have been underestimated.

Both studies were done in the lower grades of primary school, not with subject teachers. Still, they offer a lesson that matters for grades 6–9 as well: the meeting format alone is not enough. Results appear when the team works on a specific difficult topic and has good professional material on how students learn it.

## What can go wrong

- **Time.** It is often assumed that Japanese teachers have special time set aside for this. Watanabe says this is not true: they have no more free time than colleagues in other countries, and part of the work happens after hours. Our timetables are full too, so the model has to be modest.
- **Fear of being observed.** If observation feels like an inspection, the teacher will deliver a "safe" lesson and nothing will be learned. Without trust within the team, the model does not work.
- **Box-ticking.** Lesson study easily becomes one more set of minutes for the archive. The EEF result shows that a well-organised process does not in itself guarantee better attainment.
- **Small subject teams.** In smaller schools, the maths or physics teacher is often the only one. The team can then be cross-subject or cross-school, but that takes extra organisation.
- **One lesson is not proof.** Even Japanese teams do not draw final conclusions from a single lesson; it is a hypothesis to be tested.

## How to start tomorrow

1. **Choose one difficult topic in your subject team.** For example, dividing fractions in grade 6, linear equations in grade 7, or percentages in grade 8. Pick a topic where students regularly make mistakes on tests.
2. **Write a three-column plan.** The task, anticipated student responses (including typical mistakes), and the teacher's response. Just predicting mistakes together is useful, even without observation.
3. **Agree on one research lesson per term.** Two or three colleagues observing is enough. Each observer follows two or three students and notes what they write and say.
4. **Base the post-lesson discussion on evidence.** Start with "What did we see students doing?" rather than "How did it go?". Finish with one change to the plan that you will try in another class.
5. **For class teachers in grades 1–5:** teachers of the same grade can do the same with topics such as fractions in grade 4 or reading comprehension; this is exactly the age group on which the model has been tested most.
