When the only answers a child has to “How are you?” are “fine” or “bad”, it is hard for teachers and parents to understand what is really going on. A child who can tell “I'm disappointed” from “I'm angry” finds it easier to ask for help, to resolve a conflict and to get back to learning. This workshop is ready to print and takes one lesson.

## Why it is worth spending a lesson on this

Social and emotional learning (SEL) is not an add-on for “soft” topics; it is part of what makes learning possible. CASEL describes five areas of these skills, and the first is self-awareness: the ability to understand our own emotions and thoughts and how they influence behaviour.

The evidence is encouraging. A 2011 meta-analysis by Durlak and colleagues covered 213 school programmes and more than 270,000 pupils, from kindergarten to the end of secondary school. Pupils in the programmes showed better social and emotional skills and behaviour, and the studies that measured achievement recorded a gain of about 11 percentile points. A more recent meta-analysis (Cipriano and colleagues, 2023) covered 424 studies from 53 countries and more than 575,000 pupils, and confirmed improvements in skills, peer relationships, school climate and achievement.

For the idea of “feeling words” in particular, a study of Yale University's RULER programme is worth a look. It involved 273 fifth and sixth graders in three primary schools: classes that worked with feeling words over 30 weeks had better end-of-year grades and better teacher ratings of social skills. The workshop below is not the RULER programme but a stand-alone activity built on the same basic principle: a feeling is easier to understand when it has the right name.

## Workshop: Feeling families and the intensity ladder

**Grade:** 2–4 (variations for grades 1 and 5 are below).

**Duration:** 45 minutes.

**Goal:** pupils name at least three words for each of four basic feelings, rate the intensity of a feeling on a scale from 1 to 5 and name at least one way to calm down.

**Materials:**

- word cards (one set per group): *content, cheerful, thrilled; disappointed, sad, devastated; irritated, angry, furious; worried, scared, terrified*
- four large sheets of paper, each with a five-rung ladder drawn on it (rung 1 at the bottom, 5 at the top)
- situation cards (examples in step 4)
- sticky notes, markers, a box or an envelope

### Lesson plan

**1. Introduction: “Fine” is not enough (5 minutes).** Ask a few pupils how they are and write their answers on the board. “Fine” will come up most often. Say: “Today we'll learn words that let us say more precisely how we feel.” Model it yourself: “This morning I was a little irritated because the bus was late. Not much, maybe a two out of five.”

**2. Feeling families (10 minutes).** Split the class into groups of four. Each group gets a shuffled set of cards and has to sort them into four “families”: joy, sadness, anger and fear. Walk around the groups and ask: “How do you know this word belongs to this family?” Explain unfamiliar words with a short situation, not a definition.

**3. The intensity ladder (10 minutes).** Each group takes one family and sticks its words on a ladder: weaker feelings lower, stronger ones higher. Pupils can add words of their own. Look at all four ladders together briefly and stress that the same feeling can be small or big, and both are fine.

**4. What would I feel? (12 minutes).** Groups draw situation cards, for example:

- I lost my favourite pencil.
- A friend didn't ask me to play with them.
- I got top marks on my dictation.
- There's a maths test tomorrow.
- Someone pushed in front of me in the lunch queue.

The group chooses a word and a rung on the ladder and explains its choice. The key moment comes when two groups choose different feelings for the same situation. Then ask: “Can more than one feeling be right?” This is how children learn that others don't have to feel the same as they do.

**5. What helps me? (8 minutes).** Together, make a list called “What helps me when I'm on a four or a five”: deep breathing, a glass of water, talking to the teacher, a few minutes of quiet, drawing. Leave the list on the wall. At the end, every pupil writes one word on a sticky note describing how they feel right now and drops it in the box, without a name.

### Discussion questions

- What new feeling word did you learn today?
- What does your body feel like when you're angry, and when you're worried?
- Why is it useful to say “a three” instead of just “I'm angry”?
- What can you do when you notice a friend is having a hard time?

### Variations

- **Grade 1:** use drawings of faces instead of words, two words per family and a three-rung ladder. Read the situations aloud.
- **Grade 5:** add more complex words (*embarrassed, jealous, proud, relieved*) and ask pupils to identify how a character in a class reader feels, quoting a sentence from the text.
- **Lower secondary, briefly:** in a form-time lesson in grade 6 or 7, the same activity can open a conversation about test stress and the move to subject teaching.

## What can go wrong

**Children don't want to talk about themselves.** That is normal and should be respected. This is why the workshop uses made-up situations and anonymous notes. No one should be made to say how they feel.

**A child reveals something serious.** If a pupil mentions violence, neglect or long-lasting sadness, don't open it up in front of the class. Talk in private and involve the school's counsellor or psychologist according to the school's protocol. A teacher is not a therapist, and this workshop is not therapy.

**One lesson doesn't change much.** Meta-analyses suggest that the best results come from programmes that are sequenced, active, focused and explicit, and that run for longer. The words from this workshop need to keep coming back in everyday teaching, or they are soon forgotten.

**Read the numbers with care.** The achievement gain in the 2011 study comes from the smaller share of studies that measured it, and the RULER study was quasi-experimental; its authors themselves call the results preliminary. Most of the research was done in the US and other countries, not in schools like ours.

## How to use it tomorrow

1. **Print the cards** with the twelve words and the ladders, and run the workshop in a form-time or language lesson.
2. **Introduce a one-minute morning check-in:** pupils show a number from 1 to 5 with their fingers or say one word. Quietly check in later with children who are on a four or a five.
3. **Use the words in your teaching:** when reading a story, ask what feeling a character has and which rung it is on.
4. **Involve parents:** send home a short note with the ladder, asking them to ask their child at dinner “Which word best describes your day today?” instead of “How was school?”.
