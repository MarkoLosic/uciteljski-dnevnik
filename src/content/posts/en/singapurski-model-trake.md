Word problems are where many primary school children "break": they know how to calculate, but not what to calculate. Singapore, whose fourth graders have topped the world in mathematics for years, has a simple and cheap tool for this: drawing rectangles. In this article I explain how it works, what the data says about it and how to try it as soon as next week.

**In short:** the bar model is Singapore's way of solving word problems, in which students draw every quantity as a rectangle before writing any calculation. It is taught in the concrete → pictorial → abstract (CPA) sequence: objects first, then a drawing, then numbers and symbols. In TIMSS 2023, Singapore's fourth graders averaged 615 points in mathematics, the highest in the world, and attempts to transfer the approach to England produced a small but positive gain.

## Why Singapore

In the international TIMSS 2023 study, fourth graders from Singapore scored an average of 615 points in mathematics, the highest of all participants, against an international scale centre point of 500. For comparison, when Bosnia and Herzegovina first took part in TIMSS at grade 4 in 2019, its students averaged 452 points, while students from Serbia and Croatia scored around 508 and 509.

In its report on the 2023 results, Singapore's Ministry of Education also highlights something mentioned less often: its fourth graders reported more positive attitudes towards mathematics and higher confidence than four years earlier. So this is not just about drill.

## How it works: concrete → pictorial → abstract

At the heart of Singapore's primary mathematics is a sequence known as CPA (*Concrete–Pictorial–Abstract*). According to the curriculum description in the TIMSS Encyclopedia, teachers lead students from everyday situations and objects, through drawings, to numbers and symbols. Researchers at Singapore's National Institute of Education (NIE) note that the approach has been officially advocated in Singapore's schools since the early 1980s and that it goes back to psychologist Jerome Bruner's ideas about three modes of representing knowledge.

![Illustration of three steps: on the left, cubes and paper strips; in the middle, a drawing of rectangles (bars) on paper; on the right, numbers and operation signs; arrows lead from objects through the drawing to numbers](/slike/singapurski-model-trake-kocke-crtez-broj.jpg)

In practice it looks like this:

1. **Concrete:** children work with objects: cubes, sticks, counters, paper strips.
2. **Pictorial:** they draw the same relationship, usually as rectangles ("bars").
3. **Abstract:** only then do they write the operation or equation.

The key is not to skip steps too early, and for the teacher to keep connecting all three levels: "Where is this number in the drawing? Which cubes does it stand for?"

### What the bar model is

Singapore's best-known tool at the pictorial level is the **bar model**. Each quantity in the problem is drawn as a rectangle, and the length of the rectangle roughly matches its size. There are two basic forms:

- **Part–whole:** one long bar is the whole, divided into parts. Good for addition and subtraction.
- **Comparison:** two or more bars one below the other, showing the difference or how many times bigger one is. Good for "how many more" and "how many times as many" problems.

In its materials for parents, Singapore's Ministry of Education explains that the bar model helps children develop algebraic thinking in a way that is less abstract than using letters in place of numbers.

## A classroom example: fourth grade

The problem: *"A basket holds three times as many apples as pears. There are 48 pieces of fruit in total. How many apples and how many pears are there?"*

A typical mistake is 48 ÷ 3 = 16, because the child sees the word "three" and the number 48. Here is how it goes in the CPA sequence:

**Concrete (5–7 minutes):** each pair gets paper strips of equal length. One strip stands for the pears. For the apples they take three identical strips. The question: "How many strips have you laid out altogether?" The children count: four.

**Pictorial (5 minutes):** in their notebooks they draw:

```
Pears:   [    ]
Apples:  [    ][    ][    ]
         \_____ total 48 _____/
```

The teacher asks: "How many equal parts can you see? How many pieces of fruit are in one part?"

**Abstract (5 minutes):** only now do they write: 48 ÷ 4 = 12 (pears), 12 × 3 = 36 (apples), check 12 + 36 = 48.

The whole point of the drawing is that the child sees for themselves that the whole is made of four equal parts, not three, and corrects the "48 ÷ 3" mistake without the teacher having to say "that's wrong".

### Adapting it by grade

- **Grades 1 and 2:** concrete and pictorial only, numbers up to 20, then up to 100. Part–whole problems: "There are 7 birds on a branch, a few more fly in and now there are 12." Bars can be built from cubes (Lego bricks, counting cubes).
- **Grades 3 and 4:** comparison, "how many more/fewer", "how many times as many", two-step problems like the one above.
- **Grade 5:** fractions. A bar divided into equal parts shows very clearly what 3/5 of a number is and prepares the ground for percentages.
- **Lower secondary (briefly):** in grades 6 and 7 the same drawing naturally turns into an equation (one part of the bar = *x*), so the bar model can bridge primary and subject teaching if class teachers and maths teachers agree on the same notation.

## What the data says outside Singapore

It is hard to prove how much of Singapore's success comes from CPA and the bar model specifically, since results are also shaped by textbooks, teacher education, time spent learning at home and many other factors. Attempts to transfer the approach to other systems are therefore more informative.

In England, the *Mathematics Mastery* programme was tested in a randomised trial funded by the Education Endowment Foundation (EEF). According to the evaluators' report, the programme introduces a "mastery" curriculum similar to those in Shanghai and Singapore (fewer topics, covered in more depth), frequent use of objects and pictures to represent concepts, and explicit teaching of the bar model from the youngest year groups, on simple problems. Ninety primary schools took part. Year 1 pupils made on average about two months' more progress than their peers in control schools, but that effect on its own was not statistically significant. Only when combined with a similar trial in Year 7 does a small but reliable positive effect emerge.

The message for us: don't expect miracles, but in a new setting the approach brings a small, positive gain, and the tool itself (a paper strip and a pencil) costs nothing.

## What can go wrong

- **Drawing becomes an end in itself.** If a "neat" model is graded, children spend time on straight lines instead of relationships. The drawing should be a sketch.
- **Taking length too literally.** A study with Singaporean students found that some use the bars very concretely, drawing a longer bar for a bigger number even when that doesn't matter for the relationship in the problem. This is a normal stage, but it should be noticed and talked about.
- **Skipping the concrete stage.** If you go straight to drawing, weaker students just get one more "template" they don't understand.
- **Time.** At first, CPA takes more minutes per problem. With packed curricula, the teacher has to consciously choose fewer problems, explored in more depth.
- **Mismatch with home.** Parents who learned "just calculate" may tell the child that drawing isn't necessary. A short explanation at a parents' meeting helps.
- **Importing the whole package.** The bar model is not the Singapore system. Without good problems, time for discussion and teacher training, the drawing alone won't raise results.

## How to start tomorrow

1. **Pick one type of problem.** For example, "how many more" in grade 2 or "how many times as many" in grade 4. Don't introduce everything at once.
2. **Prepare paper strips.** Cut strips of equal length (e.g. 10 cm) for each pair of students. That is all the material you need for the concrete step.
3. **Introduce a "drawing first, then calculation" rule.** For a week, ask for a bar sketch before the operation in every word problem, and assess whether the drawing shows the relationship, not how it looks.
4. **Ask "where is that in the drawing?"** When a student gives an answer, have them point to the part of the bar it refers to. This is the quickest check for understanding.
5. **Send parents one example.** A short message with one solved problem (drawing + calculation) explains why the child draws and how they can practise at home without ready-made answers.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the bar model in maths?

The bar model is a drawing in which every quantity from a word problem is represented by a rectangle (a bar). The length of the bar roughly matches the size of the quantity, so the student sees the relationship (part and whole, or a difference) before writing the calculation.

### What does the CPA approach mean?

CPA (*Concrete–Pictorial–Abstract*) is a learning sequence: the student first works with objects, then draws the same relationship, and only at the end writes numbers and symbols. Singapore has officially advocated it since the early 1980s.

### From which grade can the bar model be introduced?

The concrete and pictorial steps can be introduced as early as grade 1, with part–whole problems and numbers up to 20. Comparison ("how many more", "how many times as many") comes in grades 3 and 4, and fractions in grade 5.

### Is the bar model the reason for Singapore's success?

That can't be claimed with certainty, as results are also shaped by textbooks, teacher education and other factors. A trial in England showed that a similar approach brings a small but positive gain.
