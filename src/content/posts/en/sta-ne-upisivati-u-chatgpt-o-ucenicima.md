## Introduction

Artificial intelligence can save a teacher a lot of time: suggesting tasks, rewording a letter to parents or helping with written feedback. That is exactly where the biggest risk lies. To get a "good" answer, it is tempting to paste a pupil's name, their marks, a diagnosis or a description of their family situation into the chat window.

Software testing has a simple rule: don't assume where data ends up — check. With children's data the rule is even stricter: if you're not sure where it ends up, don't send it.

## Why this matters

Once you type something into an AI tool, it is no longer only on your computer. Depending on the tool, the account and its settings, the conversation may be stored on the company's servers, reviewed by staff for quality control, or used to train future models.

Data about children — especially about their health, development, behaviour and family circumstances — is among the most sensitive personal data there is. It is protected by data protection law (in the EU and UK, the GDPR), and schools carry special responsibility for it. Parents entrusted it to the school, not to the company that makes the AI tool.

## What never to type in

- **A pupil's full name**, especially together with anything else about them.
- **Marks, test results and behaviour notes** linked to a specific child.
- **Health data and diagnoses**: developmental difficulties, attention disorders, allergies, medication.
- **Details from an individual education plan (IEP)**.
- **Family circumstances**: divorce, abuse, benefits, an absent parent.
- **Identifying details**: ID numbers, address, phone number, the school and class next to the child's name.
- **Photos of children, their work with their name on it, or voice recordings**.
- **Descriptions of conflicts and disciplinary procedures** in which the child could be recognised.

Remember: a child can be identified without a name. A sentence like "the only boy in Year 2 at a small village school whose father has moved abroad" can reveal more than the name itself.

## So how should you use AI?

AI doesn't need data about a specific child to help. It needs a description of the situation.

### 1. Generalise

Instead of a real child, describe a typical situation: "a Year 2 pupil who reads slowly and skips lines". That is enough to get ideas for practice activities.

### 2. Anonymise

If you need a more specific description, remove everything that identifies the child: replace the name with "Pupil A" and leave out the school, the town and family details.

**Bad:**

```text
Mark Peterson in 3B has ADHD and keeps disrupting lessons. His mum told me
his parents are divorcing. Write his report comment for me.
```

**Better:**

```text
Write three examples of supportive written feedback for a Year 3 pupil who
finds it hard to stay focused but is making good progress in maths.
The tone should be encouraging and specific.
```

The second request gives you something usable and reveals nothing about a real child. You add the specific details yourself, in the school's own system, not in the AI tool.

### 3. Use tools your school has approved

Some schools and education authorities have agreements with suppliers that guarantee how data is stored. If such a tool exists, use it. If not, act as if everything you type is public.

### 4. Check the settings

Many tools let you turn off the use of your conversations for model training or turn off chat history. Find that option and switch it on. It doesn't replace anonymisation, but it reduces the risk.

## Check before you send

Before you press "Send", run through five questions:

- [ ] Does the text contain the child's first name, surname or nickname?
- [ ] Could the child be recognised from the description (school, class, town, family)?
- [ ] Is there any health or other sensitive information?
- [ ] Would a parent be comfortable seeing this text?
- [ ] Do I really need a specific detail for this task?

If the answer to any of the first four questions is "yes" or "not sure", the text needs rewriting.

## Conclusion

AI can be an excellent helper in lesson preparation, but it is not the place for a pupil's file. The rule is simple: give the tool the situation, not the child.
