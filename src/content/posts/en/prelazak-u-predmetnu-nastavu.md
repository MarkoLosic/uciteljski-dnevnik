At the end of fifth grade, a child leaves the classroom where they spent five years with one class teacher, and in September they are met by a dozen teachers, new subjects and a different way of grading. Many parents see grades drop for the first time and wonder what happened to their child. Research suggests the question should also be asked differently: what happened to the system around the child.

## What changes over one summer

In the nine-year primary school in Bosnia and Herzegovina, the first five grades are taught by a single class teacher, and subject teaching starts in sixth grade. For a pupil aged eleven or twelve this means several changes at once:

- instead of one adult who knows them well, they get a teacher for each subject, and some of those teachers see the class only once or twice a week;
- every teacher has their own rules, pace and grading criteria;
- the material becomes more abstract (in maths, for example, fractions and decimals), and there is less time for practice;
- all of this happens just as puberty begins.

Psychologists Jacquelynne Eccles and Carol Midgley explain this with the theory of stage-environment fit: when school becomes more impersonal and stricter exactly when a child needs more autonomy and a close relationship with adults, motivation and achievement fall. The theory was developed in the United States and later tested in other systems, including English schools.

## What the data show

**Grades in the region.** In 2008, Željko Kraljić collected the grades of about 600 pupils from nine primary schools in three Croatian counties, at the end of fourth and at the end of fifth grade (in Croatia's eight-year school, subject teaching starts in fifth grade). The share of top and very good grades in maths fell from 70 to 46 percent, and in Croatian from 72 to 48 percent. The number of lowest passing grades in maths more than tripled. The drop is almost the same in both subjects, even though they are taught by different teachers, which suggests the problem is not one "strict" teacher but the transition itself. As possible reasons the author lists more subjects and lessons, less revision and practice, and the fact that a class teacher who has known a child for years often, without realising it, grades more than knowledge alone.

**Test scores in the US.** Jonah Rockoff and Benjamin Lockwood followed pupils in New York City from third to eighth grade. Those who moved to a separate middle school in sixth grade showed a noticeable drop on standardised tests, about 0.18 standard deviations in maths and 0.16 in English, compared with peers who stayed in a combined school up to eighth grade. Martin West and Guido Schwerdt found the same pattern in data from Florida and estimated that the drop equals a loss of three and a half to seven months of expected learning.

An important caveat: these studies measure a move to a different building and a different staff. Our nine-year school is structurally closer to schools where children stay in the same place, which, according to this data, is an advantage. Still, even within the same building almost everything else changes: the people, the rules and the way of working.

**What helps.** The English EPPSE study from 2008 surveyed more than 500 children and their parents in the first term after moving to secondary school, at around eleven years old, the age of our sixth-graders. Most children, 84 percent, felt ready, and after one term only three percent were still worried. Children who received a lot of help from their new school were more likely to have a successful transition. Useful practices named in the study include shared "bridging" materials used both before and after the move, sharing information about children, visits from future teachers, gentler rules in the first weeks, and explicitly teaching how to take notes and how to study. The best results came from schools where teachers on both sides of the transition worked closely together.

## Where the system leaves a gap

Curricula describe precisely what is learned in fifth and what in sixth grade. Far less often is it defined how knowledge about the child passes from one level to the next: who reads more slowly, who has not yet mastered the times tables, who withdraws easily, and who needs more structure. That knowledge often stays with one person, and in September the subject teachers start from zero. When each of a dozen teachers "gets to know" the class on their own, the first weeks go on tests the children are not used to, and the grades from that period stay on record.

Grading is the second weak spot. If top grades dominate in fifth grade and the criteria suddenly become stricter in sixth, the child experiences it as personal failure and the parent as a sign that something is wrong with the child. Often, however, only the criterion has changed.

## A practical example: the transition as a school project

Here is how a primary school could organise the transition, drawing on the practices described in the English study:

1. **April and May, fifth grade.** The maths and mother-tongue teachers who will take over the class teach one or two lessons in fifth grade, with the class teacher present. The children get to know the faces and the way of working, and the teacher sees the class in a familiar setting.
2. **June.** The class teacher, the school pedagogue and the future form tutor meet for half an hour and exchange short notes: what the class does well, where the gaps in the material are, which children need support. Not files or labels, but what helps the teacher plan.
3. **A "bridge" task.** The same task, for example a short reading comprehension text or a few problems with fractions, is done by the children at the end of fifth and the start of sixth grade. The teacher gets a real starting point, and the children see continuity.
4. **The first three to four weeks of sixth grade.** An agreed transition period: teachers jointly teach the children how to keep notebooks for each subject, how to plan studying when there are several tests in the same week, and how to use the timetable. Tests are coordinated so they don't all fall in the same week.
5. **End of the first quarter.** A class staff meeting devoted only to the transition: which children have the largest drop and why.

## What can go wrong

- Kraljić's data are grades, not test scores, and they refer to Croatian schools in 2008. Lower grades don't necessarily mean less knowledge; in part they may be a sign of more realistic grading.
- The American and English studies were done in different systems and involve a change of building. What can be carried over is the pattern, not the numbers.
- Fewer than half of the invited families answered the EPPSE questionnaire, so the picture may be somewhat rosier than reality.
- A "softer" transition can easily turn into lowered expectations. The same study cites earlier research showing that teachers after the transition often underestimate what pupils already know, so they repeat material and children get bored. The goal is continuity, not repeating fifth grade.
- All of this takes time that is usually not included in a teacher's workload. Without the head teacher's support, it is left to enthusiasts.
- Sharing information about children must respect data protection: share only what is needed for teaching.

## How to apply it tomorrow

1. **Head teacher and school pedagogue:** put the transition from fifth to sixth grade on the agenda of the next staff meeting and name one person responsible for it.
2. **Fifth-grade teachers:** by the end of the term, write down three strengths and three gaps in the material for your class, so they are ready to hand over in June.
3. **Subject teachers:** agree with colleagues from the primary grades on at least one lesson you will teach in fifth grade during the second term.
4. **Maths and mother-tongue departments:** create one shared "bridge" task for the end of fifth and the start of sixth grade.
5. **Parents:** tell your child in advance that a drop in some grades in sixth grade is common and does not mean they are weaker than before. Watch how they cope with the timetable and studying for several subjects, not only the grades.
