## Introduction

In IT, when we check how fast an application is, we rarely look at the average. We look at something called **p95**. When I first understood why, I thought of the classroom — because it's the same mistake we easily make when we look at children.

![A teacher working with a group of children at a table while other children read on a rug, each on their own path](/slike/prosek-zavarava.jpg)

## What is p95?

Picture a restaurant. **p95 = 3 seconds means that 95% of guests wait up to 3 seconds, and 5% wait longer.** It doesn't tell you how long the "average" guest waits — it tells you how long almost everyone waits, and it reveals that there is a group having a much worse time.

## Why the average misleads

Take a hundred guests. 99 of them wait 1 second each, and one waits 100 seconds.

- Average: (99 × 1 + 100) / 100 = **about 2 seconds**. Looks great.
- Reality: one guest waited almost two minutes and is **very unhappy**.

The average smoothed out that one bad case and hid it. On the chart everything looks fine, while someone has already got up and left.

## The same thing happens in the classroom

Swap the guests for children, and the seconds for grades or the time it takes to master a topic.

A class has an average of 4.3 in maths (on a 1–5 scale). At a parents' meeting, that sounds lovely. But in a class of twenty pupils, that 5% is **one child** — and that child may still not understand adding across ten while everyone else has moved on. The average doesn't see them. The teacher has to.

The same goes for pace. If children finish a worksheet in ten minutes "on average", that says nothing about the child who needs thirty. That's exactly the child who needs attention most, and the average systematically pushes them aside.

## What to look at instead of the average

In QA, I learned to ask one question: **who waits the longest, and why?** In the classroom, I'd translate it like this:

1. **Look at the slowest, not the average.** Who are the two or three children struggling most? What exactly are they missing?
2. **Look at the distribution, not a single number.** A class where everyone has a 4 and a class where half the children have a 5 and half a 3 have the same average — 4.0 — but completely different needs.
3. **Follow the same child over time.** Is their "tail" getting shorter — do they need less help from week to week?
4. **Be careful with reports that only give an average** — including those generated by AI progress-tracking tools. Always ask: and who's at the bottom?

## Conclusion

The average is a comfortable number. It tells us that "things are mostly fine". But in a restaurant and in a classroom alike, the one who waits longest is the one who remembers the experience — and usually the one who needs us most.

Next time you see a nice average, ask yourself: who is my p95? And who are the 5% behind it?
