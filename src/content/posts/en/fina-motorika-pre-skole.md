## Introduction

When parents think about school readiness, they usually ask whether their child knows the letters, can count to twenty and can write their own name. Far fewer ask whether the child can hold a pencil for twenty minutes without tiring, cut paper along a line or zip up their own coat.

Yet it is exactly these small things that make a big difference in the first year of school. A child who is fighting with the pencil spends all their attention on drawing the line, and there is nothing left for the letter, the word or the task itself. From the outside it looks like a lack of interest or slowness; in fact, it is the small muscles of the hand getting tired.

In this article I write about what fine motor skills are, why they matter before starting school, how to tell that a child needs more practice, and what you can do at home without any special materials.

## What are fine motor skills?

Fine motor skills are the ability to control objects with small, precise movements of the fingers and hand. They go together with hand–eye coordination: the eye guides the hand, and the hand follows what the eye sees.

Fine motor skills don't develop on their own or overnight. They are built gradually, through thousands of small movements: grasping, squeezing, tearing, threading, pouring, drawing. The three-finger pencil grip we need for writing develops in most children somewhere between the ages of four and six. Before that, children often hold the pencil in their whole fist, and that is perfectly normal.

## Why do they matter in the first year?

In the first year of school, fine motor skills sit behind almost every activity:

- **Writing.** Children learn to form letters between the lines, in the right stroke order. That takes both strength and control.
- **Drawing and art.** Colouring inside the lines, cutting with scissors, gluing.
- **Maths.** Writing numbers, working with cubes, sticks and other counting aids.
- **Independence.** Opening a lunch box, doing up buttons, tying shoelaces, packing a school bag.

When a movement is automatic, the child can think about the content. When it isn't, every line is a small effort, and a string of small efforts quickly turns into tiredness and resistance to writing.

## How to tell a child needs more practice

The signs below are not a diagnosis, just a signal that a child needs more opportunities to practise:

- holds the pencil in a fist or keeps switching hands;
- tires quickly when drawing and avoids activities with pencils and crayons;
- presses so hard the paper tears, or so lightly the line is barely visible;
- finds cutting with scissors difficult, even along a straight line;
- struggles to thread large beads or do up buttons.

If several of these signs appear together and don't ease with practice, it's worth talking to the nursery teacher, your GP or an occupational therapist. Noticing early is much easier than correcting later.

## What to do at home

The best fine motor practice doesn't look like practice. It looks like play and everyday chores:

- **Play dough and real dough.** Squeezing, rolling balls and "snakes", cutting with a plastic knife.
- **Clothes pegs.** Clipping pegs onto the edge of a box or a line strengthens the fingers used to hold a pencil.
- **Threading.** Large beads, pasta tubes or buttons on a shoelace.
- **Tearing and gluing.** Tearing paper into small pieces and making a mosaic.
- **Scissors.** First free cutting, then along a straight line, then along a curve.
- **Drawing on an upright surface.** Chalk on a board or a marker on paper taped to the wall. This position naturally puts the wrist in the right place.
- **The kitchen.** Pouring water from a jug, stirring, kneading, peeling a mandarin.
- **Blocks and puzzles.** Lego bricks, chunky puzzles, inset boards.

Ten to fifteen minutes a day is enough, as long as it's regular. A short daily game is worth far more than one long "practice session" at the weekend.

## Where do screens fit in?

This site also has a few [interactive games for preschoolers](/en/games): balloons, following a path, a maze, joining the dots. They can help with hand–eye coordination and attention, but they are **an addition, not a replacement** for working with the hands. On a screen the finger glides over glass; on paper the hand works against the resistance of the pencil, and that resistance is exactly what builds strength and control.

## Checklist: fine motor skills and independence before starting school

This list is not a test and is not for grading the child. It helps you see what already comes easily and what needs a bit more play and time.

- [ ] Holds a pencil with three fingers, without gripping with the whole hand
- [ ] Copies a circle, a cross and a square
- [ ] Colours inside a large outline, mostly without going over
- [ ] Cuts with scissors along a straight and a gently curved line
- [ ] Threads large beads onto a lace
- [ ] Does up buttons and the zip on a coat
- [ ] Opens a lunch box and a bottle independently
- [ ] Can draw or colour for about ten minutes without getting very tired
- [ ] Copies their own name in capital letters (doesn't have to be perfect)
- [ ] Completes a puzzle of around twenty pieces

If most items aren't ticked yet a few months before school starts, there's no need to panic. It's a sign that now is the right time for more play dough, scissors and clothes pegs.

## Conclusion

Fine motor skills are not an "extra" to reading and writing; they are the foundation those skills stand on. The good news is that they grow through ordinary, everyday things: play, the kitchen, drawing and a little patience.

We don't need a perfect letter at preschool age. We need a child for whom the pencil is a friend, not an obstacle.
