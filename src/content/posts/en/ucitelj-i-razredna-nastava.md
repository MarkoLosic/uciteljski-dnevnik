## Introduction

The first five years of school decide whether a child will grow to love learning or experience it as a duty to be survived. In that time, children don't just learn to read, write and count — they learn how to learn, how to work with others and how to deal with mistakes.

That's why the primary school teacher holds one of the most responsible jobs in the education system. A teacher is at once an instructor, a carer, an organiser and an observer of development — and often the first person outside the family whose approval and support a child seeks.

In this post I look at what makes primary teaching special, where the system falls short today, and in which directions we can make concrete progress.

## What sets this age apart, and the teacher's role

Children aged six to eleven learn through concrete experience, movement, play and a relationship with an adult they trust. Abstract thinking is only just developing, attention spans are short, and children in the same class can be up to two years apart in development.

This leads to four key features of primary teaching:

- **One teacher, all day.** The class teacher teaches almost every subject and spends the most time with the children. Their style, patience and expectations directly shape how a child feels about school.
- **Learning in connected wholes.** Children this age don't think in subjects. They learn best when topics are linked — for example, when science, maths and language come together in a single investigation.
- **Emotions are part of teaching.** Fear of mistakes, shame or a sense of failure quickly block learning. A safe classroom isn't an extra; it's a precondition.
- **The move to subject teaching.** Year five is the bridge to year six, when children get several teachers and different expectations. The quality of that transition is often overlooked.

So today's teacher is not just a transmitter of knowledge. They are a guide who recognises each child's needs, adjusts the pace and creates conditions in which children find answers for themselves.

## Weaknesses in teaching and in the teacher's position

The biggest problem in primary education isn't a lack of effort from teachers — it's a system that asks a lot of them and gives them little room or support. The weaknesses fall into six groups.

### 1. An overloaded curriculum focused on content

Curricula are often crammed with facts that need to be "covered". Teachers race through material instead of checking whether children have really understood. The result is shallow knowledge that is quickly forgotten, and little time for practice, revision and play.

### 2. The dominance of whole-class lecturing

In many classrooms, the model is still the teacher talking while children listen and copy. Group work, investigations and project-based learning exist, but more as the exception than the rule. Children therefore develop less independence, collaboration and critical thinking.

### 3. Grading that measures instead of helping

In the lower grades, the numerical mark often becomes a goal in itself. Parents and children chase the top grade, not progress. Formative assessment — regular feedback on what a child knows and what the next step is — is applied inconsistently.

### 4. Paperwork that eats up time

Registers, reports, plans and forms, on paper or online, take hours that a teacher could spend preparing lessons or working with a child. Teachers often say that paperwork is their biggest source of exhaustion.

### 5. Inclusion without real support

Children with developmental difficulties rightly attend mainstream classes. Yet the teacher often has no teaching assistant, special education teacher or psychologist available, and no additional training. Inclusion then stays on paper, and the burden falls on one person.

### 6. The teacher's status and professional development

Professional development is often a formality: you sit through a seminar, hand in a certificate, and practice stays the same. What's missing is mentoring, sharing experience among colleagues and time to reflect on one's own work. On top of that, the profession's status and pay don't match the responsibility teachers carry.

Add to this the uneven equipment of schools, especially between urban and rural areas, and cooperation with parents that often comes down to parents' meetings and problem-solving rather than partnership.

## Where we can make progress

Progress doesn't require a revolution — it requires a series of concrete changes at the level of the system, the school and the classroom itself. The table links each weakness to a direction for improvement.

| Weakness | Direction of progress | Who can act |
|----------|-----------------------|-------------|
| Overloaded curriculum | Less content, more depth; clear learning outcomes instead of a list of lessons | Ministry, pedagogical institute |
| Whole-class lecturing | Group work, projects, learning through play and investigation | Teacher, primary teachers' team |
| Grading | Descriptive and formative assessment, portfolios, self-assessment | Teacher, school, legislators |
| Paperwork | Simplified, digitised records without duplication | Ministry, school management |
| Inclusion | Teaching assistants, a support team in every school, training for working with children with developmental difficulties | School founder, local community |
| Professional development | Mentoring, model lessons, peer exchange instead of formal seminars | School, pedagogical institute |

### In the classroom, starting tomorrow

Many changes don't need to wait for reform. A teacher can introduce them right away:

- **Short activity cycles.** A lesson split into 10–15 minute blocks, with changes of activity and movement, suits the attention span of children this age better.
- **Linking subjects by theme.** One topic, such as "Water" or "My town", can be explored through language, maths, science and art.
- **Feedback instead of just a grade.** A sentence like "Your calculation is right — now check the units" helps more than a number.
- **Mistakes are part of learning.** A mistake is treated as a step in learning, not a failure. That builds confidence and the willingness to try again.
- **Reading as a daily habit.** Reading aloud every day and talking about what was read builds comprehension, vocabulary and a love of books.
- **Thoughtful use of technology.** Digital tools make sense when children create, explore or practise with them — not when they just stare passively at a screen.

### Partnership with parents

Parents are allies, not just an audience for a report card. Short, regular progress updates, open lessons and shared activities build trust. When a child sees that the teacher and the parent are pulling in the same direction, motivation grows.

### Looking after the teacher

An exhausted teacher can't create a stimulating classroom. Less paperwork, support from a specialist team, smaller classes and public recognition of the profession are not luxuries. They are investments that pay off directly in the quality of teaching.

## Conclusion

Primary education is the foundation on which all other learning is built, and the teacher is its most important pillar. The weaknesses we see — from overloaded curricula and lecturing to paperwork and inclusion without support — are not inevitable.

Real change begins when the system gives teachers back their time and trust, and teachers invest that time in children. The goal isn't for children to know as many facts as possible by the end of year five, but to move on to year six curious, independent and confident.
