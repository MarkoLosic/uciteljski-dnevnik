When a child [moves into sixth grade](/en/blog/prelazak-u-predmetnu-nastavu), there is more homework, there are more subjects, and parents increasingly feel unsure how to help. Many sit down next to their child and work through the assignments together, assuming that more help is always better. Over the past twenty years, research has pointed to something different: for pupils in the middle grades, *how* a parent helps matters far more than *how much*.

![A boy sits at a desk under the warm light of a lamp working on maths problems in his notebook, while his mother stands beside him smiling with an open, supportive gesture without taking the pencil](/slike/domaci-zadatak-predmetna-nastava.jpg)

## What the research says

First, homework does not work the same way at every age. A 2006 research synthesis by Harris Cooper and colleagues at Duke University showed that the link between time spent on homework and academic achievement is stronger for older students (from seventh grade upwards) than for younger ones. Cooper also highlights the so-called ten-minute rule: roughly ten minutes of homework per grade level, which for sixth grade means about an hour a day across all subjects combined, not per subject. He warns, too, that overloading students with homework does not bring better grades even for older pupils.

Second, parental help on its own is no magic wand. In 2008, Patall, Cooper and Robinson pooled studies on parent involvement in homework and concluded that training parents helps children finish assignments more often and have fewer homework problems, yet the effect on achievement itself is negligible except among the youngest pupils. In observational studies that simply followed families over time, the association for middle-school-aged children was even negative, while setting clear rules (when and where homework is done) worked best.

Third, in 2009 Nancy Hill and Diana Tyson analysed 50 studies on parental involvement in American middle schools, roughly corresponding to grades 6 to 8. Almost every form of involvement was positively associated with achievement — except direct help with homework. The strongest link appeared for what the authors call academic socialisation: talking with the child about why learning matters, connecting schoolwork to the child's interests and goals, and helping them plan how to study.

The largest and most recent review was published by Jianzhong Xu and colleagues in 2024 in the journal *Psicothema*: 28 studies covering more than 378,000 participants in total. Overall, parental homework help had a weak negative relationship with achievement. Once the authors separated different types of help, however, the picture changed. Only **autonomy support** — listening to the child's ideas, encouraging them to try on their own, and helping them find their own way — was positively linked to achievement. Control, close monitoring, frequent intrusion, and directly explaining subject content showed no positive association.

Individual longitudinal and large-scale studies with lower-secondary pupils point in the same direction. In a study by Moroni and colleagues (2015) with 1,685 eighth graders, frequent and intrusive help went hand in hand with lower achievement, whereas supportive involvement went with higher results. Xu and colleagues (2018) followed 336 ninth graders: children whose parents had earlier offered more autonomy support went on to achieve better results later, while the reverse held where parents focused more on explaining content directly.

## An example: the same homework, two evenings

Imagine a seventh-grade girl with maths homework: five problems on ratios and proportions. She gets stuck on the third one.

![Split illustration: on the left in cool grey tones, a tense mother leans over the desk and writes the solution into the notebook herself while the child looks away dejectedly; on the right in warm tones, a smiling mother talks with the child, who holds the pencil and thinks over open notebooks](/slike/domaci-zadatak-dvije-veceri.jpg)

**First version.** Her mum sits down beside her, takes the pencil, and says, "Let me show you." She solves the problem her own way — different from the method the teacher used in class — then checks the other four and fixes the mistakes. Homework is finished in ten minutes, but on tomorrow's test the girl has no idea how to start, because someone else did the thinking.

**Second version.** Her mum stays standing and asks, "What exactly is the question asking for? Which example did you do in class? Where did you get stuck?" Once the girl flips through her notebook and finds a similar worked example, her mum suggests she try on her own first — and if it still does not work, circle the problem and ask the teacher in the morning. Homework takes longer and may not look spotless, but the pupil knows what she does not yet understand, and the teacher gets an honest picture of what the class missed.

The difference is not whether a parent is present, but who does the mental work.

## How this shifts by age

- **Fifth and sixth grade (the transition):** children still need plenty of structure. A parent helps set up a schedule, organise notebooks, and keep track of assignments, while gradually stepping back from solving the tasks themselves.
- **Seventh and eighth grade:** the child plans the week independently, while the parent asks questions and gives occasional reminders. Content help happens only when the child asks for it.
- **Ninth grade:** the conversation shifts towards goals (choosing a secondary school, exploring interests). That is precisely the academic socialisation Hill and Tyson highlight.

## Limitations

Almost all of the studies above are correlational: they show an association, not a cause. Part of the negative correlation is likely explained by the fact that parents step in more often when children are already struggling — so help follows lower grades rather than causing them. Most studies were conducted in the United States, Western Europe and East Asia, and American middle school does not map neatly onto our nine-year primary school system. Comparable studies from Bosnia and Herzegovina and the wider region hardly exist.

Autonomy support is also not the same as stepping away completely. Children with learning difficulties, children returning after long absences, or pupils with major gaps in prior knowledge sometimes need patient, direct help — ideally coordinated with the school. Finally, all of this assumes that the homework itself is reasonable: if a child sits over assignments for three hours every evening, the problem lies not in parenting style, but in the amount or design of the homework.

## How to apply it tomorrow

![Infographic titled "Kako pomoći oko domaćeg?" (How to help with homework?) with three reminders translated in the numbered steps below: 1. Ask instead of solving, 2. Set rules rather than surveillance, 3. Connect learning to goals](/slike/domaci-zadatak-kako-pomoci.jpg)

1. **For parents: replace "let me show you" with questions.** "What is the problem asking? Where did you cover this in class? What have you tried so far?" If your child is still stuck, let them circle the task and ask the teacher. That is useful feedback for the school, not a failure.
2. **For parents: agree on rules, not surveillance.** A regular time and place for homework, phone out of the room, a short break after school. Rules are agreed once rather than policed every evening.
3. **For parents: talk about the "why", not only about grades.** Once a week, ask what was interesting at school, what your child might enjoy doing later on, and how particular subjects connect to that.
4. **For subject teachers: assign homework a pupil can do independently,** and coordinate total volume across the class teaching team — parents see the sum of all subjects, not just yours.
5. **For sixth-grade form tutors: use the parents' evening.** Five minutes on the difference between supporting and taking over, paired with two or three concrete questions parents can ask at home, is worth far more than generic advice to "keep an eye on your child".
