By sixth or seventh grade, most pupils already bring a smartphone to school, and every subject teacher deals with the question "what about the phones?" in every lesson, whether they think about it or not. Recent research sends a fairly clear message: the problem is not the device itself, but uncontrolled use. This post explains what the data actually say and how to set rules that work in a class of 25.

## What the data say

**PISA 2022.** In the PISA 2022 cycle, the OECD tested 15-year-olds, the age at the end of primary and the start of secondary school in Bosnia and Herzegovina. Across OECD countries, 65% of pupils said they were distracted by their own use of digital devices in at least some maths lessons. Pupils who were distracted by other pupils using devices in at least some lessons scored 15 points lower in maths than those for whom this hardly ever happened, even after accounting for socio-economic differences. Around 45% of pupils said they feel nervous when their phone is not nearby.

The same study has another side: pupils who used digital devices *for learning* at school for up to one hour a day scored 14 points higher than those who did not use them at all. The OECD's conclusion is that moderate use of technology can help, but only when it supports learning rather than pulling attention away from it. An important caveat: these are correlations, not proof of cause and effect.

**UNESCO.** The 2023 Global Education Monitoring (GEM) Report on technology in education took the position that technology belongs in the classroom only when it clearly supports learning outcomes. According to an update from the GEM team, by the end of 2024, 79 education systems (around 40%) had some form of smartphone ban in schools, most of them aimed specifically at primary school. GEM also cites research from 14 countries showing that the mere proximity of a phone with notifications is distracting, and a study finding that pupils can need up to 20 minutes to refocus after a distraction.

**SMART Schools (England).** A team from the University of Birmingham compared 1,227 pupils from 30 English secondary schools, 20 of which banned recreational phone use during the day and 10 of which allowed it during breaks. The results, published in 2025 in *The Lancet Regional Health – Europe*, showed no difference between these schools in mental wellbeing, sleep, attainment in English and maths, or classroom disruption. The ban reduced phone use at school by about 40 minutes, but total screen time stayed high, averaging four to six hours a day. On the other hand, the researchers did confirm a link between more total phone time and lower attainment, worse sleep and more disruption in class.

**What does this mean for primary school?** The data mostly come from pupils aged 12 to 15, so they apply most directly to the subject-teaching years (grades 6 to 9 in our system). The message is twofold: a phone buzzing on the desk really does harm attention, but a school-wide ban alone does not solve everything. What a teacher controls is the structure of their own lesson: when the phone is put away, when it is a tool, and who decides.

## Example: the "three states" rule

One practical model a teacher can introduce on their own, without waiting for a staff decision, is that during a lesson the phone is always in one of three clearly announced states:

1. **Away (the default).** The phone is switched off or on silent, in a bag or in a box on the teacher's desk. Not on the desk, not even face down, because the mere proximity of the device is distracting.
2. **Tool (announced and limited).** The teacher writes the task, the app and the time limit on the board, e.g. "8 minutes, camera and stopwatch only".
3. **Returned (end of activity).** At the teacher's signal, phones go back to state 1, and only then does the discussion begin.

**Sample lesson – physics, grade 7, topic: motion and speed.** In groups of three, pupils time how long it takes a ball to roll down a sloping board from three different heights. The phone is in "tool" mode for 10 minutes: one pupil times it with a stopwatch, another records slow-motion video to check the measurement, and the third writes the results in a notebook. Then the phones are put away, and the groups calculate the average speed on paper and compare results. Here the phone had a clear purpose that would be harder to achieve without it (precisely measuring a short time), and it replaced neither thinking nor calculating.

The same pattern works in other subjects: in biology for photographing leaves in the schoolyard to classify later, in geography for a compass app during orientation, in foreign languages for recording and listening back to one's own pronunciation.

**Adapting to age.** In sixth grade, when pupils are just [moving into subject teaching](/en/blog/prelazak-u-predmetnu-nastavu), the "tool" state should be rare and very short, with one device per group. In eighth and ninth grade, pupils can help write the class rules, which makes them more likely to follow them. In the lower primary grades, personal phones are generally not needed in lessons; if technology is used, it is better to use school devices under the teacher's supervision.

## What can go wrong

- **Inequality.** Not every pupil has a phone, the same model or mobile data. An activity that requires every pupil's personal phone excludes children from poorer families. That is why "one device per group" is the safer rule.
- **Privacy and photos.** Filming other pupils without permission can easily end up in a group chat or on social media. The rule should state explicitly what may be filmed (the object, the experiment) and what may not (other pupils' faces). Many social networks and AI tools require users to be at least 13 under their terms of use, some even older, so they should not be brought into lessons just because pupils already have them.
- **Inconsistency.** If one teacher allows phones on desks and another confiscates them, pupils learn that rules depend on mood rather than purpose. A rule works best when it is agreed at least by the subject department or the class's teaching team.
- **Confiscation as conflict.** Taking a phone away in front of the class often turns the lesson into an argument. A predictable routine (a box at the door, phones returned at the end) costs less time and energy.
- **Limits of the evidence.** PISA measures association, not cause, and SMART Schools was carried out in England, in a different school context. None of these data tell you how much a specific rule will help your particular class.

Before introducing any rule, check what your school's code of conduct and the regulations in your canton or entity say about phone use.

## How to start tomorrow

1. **Set the default state.** At the start of the lesson, say in one sentence where phones go ("in your bag, on silent") and be consistent for the whole week.
2. **Announce every use on the board.** Task, permitted app, time limit. Without that, the phone is not a tool but a distraction with permission.
3. **Plan for one device per group.** This avoids inequality and reduces the temptation to check messages "along the way".
4. **Agree on rules with the class.** In seventh, eighth and ninth grade, set aside ten minutes in form time for pupils to propose rules and consequences themselves; you keep the final say.
5. **Involve parents.** At the next parents' meeting, briefly explain the rule and the finding that total phone time, at home too, affects sleep and attainment. School alone cannot solve what happens after 2 p.m.
