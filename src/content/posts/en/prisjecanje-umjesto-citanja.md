When a child in third or fourth grade "studies for a test", it usually means reading the lesson in the textbook one more time, sometimes twice. It looks diligent, but research shows it is one of the weaker ways to learn. Much more comes from closing the book and trying to recall what was learned, provided that, at this age, someone helps the recall actually succeed.

## Why rereading is deceptive

Rereading creates a feeling that the material has been mastered: the text is familiar, the sentences "sound" right. But recognising something is not the same as recalling it in front of a blank sheet during a test. In a large review of ten learning techniques, Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing and distributed practice as high-utility techniques, and rereading and highlighting as low-utility ones. Most of that evidence, however, comes from work with older students and university students, so the question of what happens with primary pupils matters a great deal.

Retrieval practice means that a pupil, without looking at the material, tries to pull from memory what they have learned: answer a question, complete a sentence, fill in a diagram or retell the lesson. It is not about grading. The learning happens in that very effort to bring something back.

## What research with primary pupils says

A team from Purdue University (Jeffrey Karpicke and colleagues) ran a series of experiments in fourth-grade classes at a public elementary school in the US, with children aged 9 to 11 and texts from their science textbooks. Two findings are especially useful in practice.

**The first finding is sobering.** When pupils read a short text and were then given a blank sheet with the instruction to write down everything they remembered, they wrote on average only about 7% of the ideas in the text, and about 9% after one more reading. University students in an almost identical procedure write down about two thirds of the ideas. Building a concept map with the textbook open did no better: the children transferred only about a fifth of the content to the map. This kind of "bare" recall brought no visible benefit on a test four days later. The authors conclude that primary pupils need guided retrieval.

**The second finding shows what that can look like.** In the third experiment, the children got a "question map": the topic title in the middle of the page and five boxes around it with specific questions. They first filled in the map with the text in front of them, and then once more without it. For another text, they spent the same time rereading. On a later free-recall task, the children remembered about 40% of the ideas from the text they had practised with the question map, and about 28% from the text they had only reread.

In another study by the same team (2016), fourth graders learned word lists and, during retrieval practice, received help in the form of a category and the first two letters of the word (e.g. "fruit: ba___"). This way the children successfully recalled three quarters of the words or more, and on the final test they remembered more than when they had only looked at the words again (55% versus 44% in the first experiment). Important for teachers: the benefit was about the same for children with weaker and stronger reading comprehension.

The message is simple: retrieval works with primary pupils too, but only if it is demanding enough that the child has to make an effort and supported enough that they succeed.

## A classroom example: a question map in a science lesson

Say a fourth-grade class is covering the water cycle. This is an illustrative example, modelled on the procedure from the study.

1. At the end of the lesson, the teacher hands out a sheet with the title "The water cycle" in the middle and five boxes around it: *What happens to water when the sun warms it? How do clouds form? When does it snow instead of rain? Where does water go after it rains? Why is the water cycle important for plants and people?*
2. Pupils fill in the map with the textbook, for about five minutes.
3. The next day, or two or three days later, at the start of the lesson everyone gets the same blank sheet and fills it in without the textbook, for two to three minutes.
4. The answers are checked together, and everyone adds what they were missing.
5. Before the test, the map goes home as a study tool: the child covers the answers and tries to recall them.

Once the child fills in all the boxes with ease, the next step is less support: fewer questions, then only key words, and finally a blank sheet with just the title.

## Adapting it to age

- **Grades 1 and 2:** spoken and short. "Close your eyes: which three animals did we talk about today?" Pictures with blank labels, completing words from the first letter, pointing at a picture.
- **Grades 3 to 5:** question maps, gap-fill sentences, cards with a question on one side and the answer on the other. Support is reduced gradually.
- **Grades 6 to 9:** pupils write their own questions from the lesson, do short ungraded quizzes at the start of class and study from their own cards. Here too it pays for the teacher to show what a good question looks like, because even older pupils do not naturally write questions that require understanding.

## At home: what parents can do

Instead of "Have you learned it?" or "Read it once more", a parent can ask three to five questions about the lesson while the book is closed. If the child gets stuck, help comes step by step: the first letter of the answer, a choice between two answers, or a look at a picture in the textbook. Afterwards, they open the book together and check what was missed. Short sessions of about ten minutes, spread over several days before the test, are in line with what research reviews say about distributed practice, and are usually easier on the child than a long read the night before.

## What can go wrong

- **A task that is too hard.** A blank sheet and "write everything you know" means, for most younger pupils, that they will recall little, feel like they failed, and learn no more than by reading. That is exactly what the first experiment with fourth graders showed.
- **A task that is too easy, right after reading.** If the child just repeats an answer they saw a moment ago, success is complete but the benefit is small. The study's authors stress that retrieval has to require the child to "go back" to earlier learning, so it is good to leave at least a short gap.
- **A quiz that turns into a grade.** If every retrieval exercise is graded, anxiety rises and the purpose is lost. Keep it as practice, not assessment.
- **Only factual questions.** A Dutch study with third and fourth graders (Ophuis-Cox and colleagues, 2024) found that, for text comprehension, summarising and rereading gave better results than questions asking only for facts. Questions should therefore include "why" and "how" as well, and retrieval should not crowd out conversation about the text.
- **The evidence is still limited.** Most studies with primary pupils are short, done in a small number of schools and outside our region. A review by Moreira and colleagues (2019) notes that in real classrooms there is not yet enough evidence that retrieval is always better than other activities teachers already use, because it was mostly compared with rereading or with "nothing".
- **Children for whom writing is draining.** For pupils with reading and writing difficulties, an oral version or circling answers is often fairer than a written response.

## How to start tomorrow

1. For the next lesson you teach, write five short questions and arrange them around the topic title on a single sheet.
2. At the end of the lesson, have pupils fill in the map using the textbook; at the start of the next lesson, have them fill it in again, without the textbook, in two or three minutes and without a grade.
3. Check the answers together straight away, so nobody walks away with a wrongly remembered fact.
4. Send parents a short message: three questions from the lesson to talk about at home, with a suggestion to help with the first letter or a choice of answers instead of rereading.
5. Before the test, reduce the support: fewer questions on the sheet, then only key words, then a blank sheet with the title.
