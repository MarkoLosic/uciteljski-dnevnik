## Introduction

When I taught first graders at Grahovo Primary School, most of my time didn't go into the lessons themselves but into everything around them: preparing worksheets, coming up with tasks for the children who had already finished and for those who needed one more try, writing notes to parents. Today I work as a QA engineer, and part of my job is evaluating AI models, so I often wonder how much a good assistant would have helped me back then — and where it would only have got in the way.

This isn't an advert for artificial intelligence. It's an attempt to answer a simple question: what can AI realistically bring to primary teaching, from first to fifth grade?

## Rule number one: AI is a tool for the teacher, not for the child

Children aged six to eleven are only just learning to read, to check information and to tell "true" apart from "sounds convincing". Most popular AI chatbots, according to their own terms of use, aren't even meant for children of this age. That's why I think the healthiest approach is this: **the teacher uses AI to prepare a better lesson, and the children work with material the teacher has checked.**

That doesn't mean children can never see how AI works. A whole-class activity at the board, where the teacher asks a question and the class discusses the answer, can be a great lesson in critical thinking. But a child "chatting" with a chatbot on their own at this age isn't a good idea.

## Where AI really helps

### 1. The same task at three levels

Every class has children who solve a task in two minutes and children who need help with the very first sentence. Differentiation is one of the most important things in primary teaching — and one of the most time-consuming.

This is where AI really saves time. For example:

> Write 5 word problems on addition and subtraction up to 20 for second grade. Make three versions: easier (smaller numbers, one step), standard and harder (two steps). Use situations from a child's everyday life: the shop, the playground, the school snack.

Within a minute you have a starting point that you then adapt to your class. The key word is *adapt* — more on that below.

### 2. Reading texts tailored to the child

For first and second graders it's hard to find short texts that are both interesting and simple enough. AI can write an 80-word story that uses the letter you're currently learning, or rewrite the same text about autumn fruit at two levels of difficulty. For a child who reads more slowly, that's the difference between "I can't" and "I did it".

### 3. Ideas for games, revision and group work

A revision quiz for science, a "guess the word" game, ten questions for morning circle, riddles for the end of a lesson — AI is an excellent brainstorming partner. Its suggestions aren't always the best, but it gives you plenty of them, and out of ten usually two or three are worth using.

### 4. Support for children who need more

For pupils on an individual education plan, AI can help break the material down into smaller steps, rewrite instructions in shorter, clearer sentences or create a visual timetable of activities. Decisions about what a child needs are still made by the teacher, the support staff and the parents — AI only speeds up preparing the materials.

### 5. Less paperwork, more time for the children

A note to parents about a school trip, a plan for next week, a short description of activities for a report — AI can draft all of these in a few seconds. You read, correct and send. The time you save goes where it should: to the children.

## A few ideas by grade

| Grade | Example use |
|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Short texts with the letters being learned, picture worksheets for counting |
| 2 | Word problems at three levels of difficulty, short dictations |
| 3 | Reading comprehension questions, from easier to harder |
| 4 | Science revision quizzes, ideas for simple experiments |
| 5 | Planning a small research project, help with structuring a composition |

## Where to be careful

As a QA engineer I've learned one rule: **don't trust a tool until you've checked it.** With AI, that goes double.

- **AI makes mistakes, and it makes them confidently.** It can get a sum wrong, invent a "fact" about an animal or write a sentence that isn't grammatically correct. Everything that goes in front of children has to pass through your hands.
- **Language.** AI sometimes mixes language variants or slips in a word children have never heard. For first graders who are just learning their letters, that matters.
- **Children's privacy.** Never enter pupils' names, grades, health information or family circumstances into an AI tool. If you need an example, write "a pupil who reads more slowly", not a name.
- **The curriculum and textbook are still the foundation.** AI doesn't know what you did last week or how your textbook is written. Tasks follow the curriculum, not the other way round.
- **A screen isn't the goal.** In primary school, children learn with their hands, through play, conversation and movement. If AI helps you prepare a better screen-free lesson — even better.

## A quick check before material goes to class

- [ ] Have I checked every calculation and every fact?
- [ ] Are the language and vocabulary right for the age group?
- [ ] Does the task follow what we actually did in class?
- [ ] Have I removed all of the children's personal data?
- [ ] Would I give this to a child even if I didn't know AI had written it?

## Conclusion

AI won't replace the teacher in primary school — what children of this age need most is patience, warmth and someone who knows them. But it can take over part of the work that keeps you away from them: preparation, rewriting, coming up with ten versions of the same task.
