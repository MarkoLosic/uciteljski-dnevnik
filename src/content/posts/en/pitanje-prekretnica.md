The question "Is that clear to everyone?" almost always gets the same answer: silence or a few nods. The teacher moves on, and only at the next test discovers that half the class misunderstood the key idea. This guide explains one simple formative assessment technique – the hinge question – that gets an answer from every pupil, in the middle of the lesson, while something can still be done about it.

## What a hinge question is

The term comes from the work of Dylan Wiliam, one of the best-known researchers on formative assessment. The idea is that a lesson has a "hinge" somewhere in the middle: the moment when the teacher has to decide whether to move on or go back over the material. At that point the teacher asks one carefully designed question, usually multiple choice, and gets an answer from every pupil at once.

In a 2015 article, Wiliam sets out a few principles: every pupil must respond, not just those who put their hand up; the question should be quick to answer, in about two minutes or less; the teacher should be able to read the responses on the spot; and, most importantly, the question must be designed so that a pupil cannot reach the right answer through wrong thinking. That is why the wrong options are not random – each one matches a common misconception.

The advantage is that the decision is made immediately. No exercise books to collect, no marking in the evening. The teacher looks around the room and within half a minute knows what to do with the rest of the lesson.

## What the research says

In 1998, Paul Black and Dylan Wiliam published a review of hundreds of studies on classroom assessment. They concluded that strengthening formative assessment produces significant and often substantial learning gains, in studies ranging from five-year-olds to university students. Particularly important was their finding that lower-attaining pupils benefited most, so the gaps within a class narrowed. At the same time, they warned that formative assessment is not a magic solution and that rushed implementation does not deliver results.

The UK's Education Endowment Foundation (EEF) estimates in its evidence review that high-quality feedback adds on average about six months of extra progress over a school year. According to the same review, the effect is somewhat larger for primary pupils than for older ones, and verbal feedback does slightly better than written feedback. These figures are averages across many studies and should be read as a direction, not a promise.

The largest test in real schools was the EEF trial of the *Embedding Formative Assessment* programme in 140 schools in England, with around 25,000 pupils. It is worth saying that these pupils were 14 to 16 years old, older than our primary pupils. Pupils in the programme schools made the equivalent of two months' additional progress, although no separate effect was found for maths and English on their own. According to preliminary findings, lower-attaining pupils made more progress. What can be carried over to primary school: the programme was not a single technique but regular monthly meetings of teachers who introduce and try out these techniques together. That is exactly the part that is easy to overlook.

## A classroom example: maths, year 6 (age 11–12)

The teacher is working on comparing decimals. Halfway through the lesson, they write on the board:

> **Which number is the largest?**\
> A) 0.25\
> B) 0.3\
> C) 0.125\
> D) They are all equal

Each answer tells you something:

- **A** is chosen by a pupil who compares the digits after the decimal point as whole numbers ("25 is more than 3").
- **C** is chosen by a pupil who thinks a longer number is bigger.
- **D** may be a sign that the pupil does not understand place value.
- **B** is the correct answer.

Pupils get 30 to 60 seconds to think in silence, then on a signal they all hold up a card with a letter at the same time. If more than roughly four-fifths of the class chose B, the teacher moves on and works briefly with the few others while the rest get on with tasks. If many pupils chose A, the teacher knows exactly what to revisit – the place value of tenths and hundredths – instead of re-teaching the whole lesson. The threshold of around 80 per cent is not a rule from research but a practical guide; for foundations that later material builds on, the bar should be higher.

## Another example: biology, year 6 or 7

> **Where does a green plant get its food from?**\
> A) From the soil, through its roots\
> B) It makes it itself in its leaves, with the help of light\
> C) From the water it absorbs\
> D) From fertiliser

Answer A is one of the most stubborn misconceptions about plants, and not only among children. If a third of the class chooses it, the story of photosynthesis has not "landed", even if pupils can recite the definition by heart.

## How to write a good hinge question

1. **Start from the misconception, not from the right answer.** Think about what pupils get wrong in this lesson year after year. Those are your wrong options.
2. **Two or three wrong answers are enough.** More options only slow down reading the room.
3. **Check whether it can be guessed.** If the right answer stands out by length or style, or is "too obvious", the question does not measure understanding.
4. **Decide in advance what you will do** for each possible outcome: move on, briefly revisit with part of the group, or revisit with everyone.
5. **Keep the tools simple:** A, B, C, D cards (one sheet per pupil, usable all year), small mini-whiteboards and markers, or fingers (one finger for A, two for B and so on).

## Adapting it for lower primary

In grades 3–5 (ages 8–11) the technique works just as well, but with less text. Instead of four written answers you can use three pictures or three written numbers, and pupils respond with coloured cards. Before the first use it is worth practising the routine itself, especially the rule "we all hold up at the same time", so that children do not look at what their deskmate chose. In grades 1 and 2 it is better to use questions with two or three answers and thumbs up or down, followed by a quick check with individual children.

## What can go wrong

- **One question is not a verdict.** The answer to a single question can be a fluke. A hinge question is information for the next step in the lesson, not the basis for a grade. If it is used for grading, pupils start copying and guessing, and the technique loses its point.
- **A badly designed question gives false confidence.** If the right answer can be reached through wrong thinking, the class looks ready when it is not.
- **Writing them takes time.** A good question is not invented five minutes before the lesson. That is why the technique is strongest when teachers of the same subject write and share questions together, for example in their department.
- **You need a plan B.** If the result shows that half the class did not understand, and the teacher moves on anyway to keep up with the scheme of work, the whole exercise was wasted.
- **Digital tools are not necessary.** Voting apps can speed up collecting answers, but they require devices and often accounts, so check age restrictions and what happens to children's data. Paper cards do the same job.

## How to start tomorrow

1. Pick one lesson next week and one concept that, in your experience, pupils most often misunderstand.
2. Write one question with three or four answers, where each wrong answer matches one specific misconception.
3. Print or cut out A–D cards for the whole class and practise the rule of holding them up together.
4. Before the lesson, write down what you will do if most pupils answer correctly, and what you will do if a large part of the class picks a particular wrong answer.
5. After the lesson, show the question to a colleague in your department and improve it together. Collect good questions in a shared bank for the subject.
