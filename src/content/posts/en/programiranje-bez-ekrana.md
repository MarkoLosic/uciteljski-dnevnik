When people talk about technology in school, the conversation quickly turns to tablets, apps and equipment that many schools in Bosnia and Herzegovina simply don't have. But the most important thing children need to learn about computers is not where to click. It is how a computer "thinks": step by step, following exact rules. In primary school this can be taught with paper, cards and chalk, and there is good evidence for it.

## What "computational thinking" actually is

Computational thinking is a set of problem-solving skills: breaking a big task into smaller parts, recognising patterns, leaving out details that don't matter and writing a clear sequence of instructions (an algorithm). It also includes finding the mistake in a set of instructions, what programmers call *debugging*.

None of these skills needs a screen. A child explaining to a classmate how to get from the desk to the door, or looking for the rule in the sequence red-blue-blue-red, is already doing what a programmer does, just without a keyboard. That idea is the basis of the approach known as *unplugged*.

## What the research says

The best-known source of materials is **CS Unplugged**, a project of the University of Canterbury in New Zealand. Its activities are free and have been used in classrooms around the world for years. The binary numbers activity even has a version labelled for Bosnia and Herzegovina, so the material doesn't have to be translated from scratch.

Several studies carried out in primary schools suggest that the approach makes sense:

- **Spain and Brazil (Brackmann et al., 2017):** primary pupils who took part in unplugged activities improved significantly more on a computational thinking test than their peers in the control groups.
- **The Netherlands (Bosgoed and Fanchamps, 2022):** 108 children aged five to seven had five 45-minute lessons, one group with unplugged programming board games, the other with ScratchJr on a tablet. The unplugged group made more progress on average, but the difference only became statistically significant once the authors took the children's age and prior knowledge into account. So the result is promising, but not conclusive.
- **Germany (Leifheit et al., 2018):** an 18-lesson course for 33 pupils in grades 3 and 4 started with games using everyday objects (pencils, playing cards) and only later moved to the computer. The authors report benefits of this approach for both understanding and children's interest.

One more thing matters: most of these studies are small and short. It's fair to say that "unplugged" is not a magic solution, but a good and cheap way into the topic, especially for the youngest pupils.

## In practice: two lessons for primary school

### Grades 1–2: "The robot teacher"

- **Goal:** children understand that a computer does exactly what we tell it, no more and no less.
- **Time:** one lesson (45 minutes).
- **Materials:** chalk or masking tape for a 5 × 5 grid on the floor (or on the board), arrow cards (forward, turn left, turn right), one toy or picture as the "goal".

**Steps:**

1. The teacher plays the robot and explains the rule: "I only know three commands and I do exactly what they say."
2. In groups, children lay out arrow cards to bring the "robot" to the goal.
3. The robot follows the commands literally. If the children missed an arrow, the robot bumps into a "wall", the class laughs, and that's a good thing.
4. The group looks for the mistake in the row of cards and fixes it. This is the key moment of the lesson: a mistake is not something to be ashamed of, it's part of the job.
5. At the end the roles switch: one child is the robot, another is the programmer.

**Questions for discussion:** Why didn't the robot "get" what we meant? Is there a shorter route? How did we find the mistake?

The lesson links easily to the subjects *Moja okolina* / *Priroda i društvo* (the early-years science and social studies subjects in BiH: orientation in space, left and right) and to PE.

### Grades 3–5: "Count the dots" (binary numbers)

- **Goal:** pupils understand how a computer writes numbers using only two states, on and off.
- **Materials:** five cards with 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 dots (they can be drawn on A4 paper), with blank backs.

**Steps:**

1. Five pupils stand in front of the board, each holding one card, ordered from 16 down to 1.
2. The rule: a card showing its dots is "1", a card showing its back is "0".
3. The teacher asks for a number, for example 13, and the class agrees which cards to turn over (8 + 4 + 1).
4. Pupils write the result in zeros and ones: 01101.
5. A challenge for fast finishers: what is the biggest number we can show with five cards? What do you notice about the number of dots on each next card?

This is as much a maths lesson as a computing one: children practise addition and place value, and along the way discover that the number of dots doubles each time.

**For older pupils (grades 6–9):** the same principle leads to writing letters as numbers and to a simple "secret message", and then to the first steps in visual programming on a computer.

## What can go wrong

- **Play without talk.** If children only lay out cards and nobody asks "why does this work?", you get fun without learning. The most valuable part of the lesson is the short discussion at the end.
- **Unplugged forever.** Working without screens is an entry point, not the goal. The German study deliberately moved from games to work on the computer; a good plan for older grades has both parts.
- **Too abstract, too early.** Binary numbers are usually too hard for first graders. Movement, arrows and patterns suit younger children better.
- **An unsure teacher.** A Slovenian study with primary teachers found that they most easily recognise algorithmic thinking, patterns and debugging, but need structured support to assess these skills. Don't expect everything to go smoothly from the first lesson.
- **Competitive pressure.** Tasks like those from the Bebras challenge (known in the region as "Dabar", the local word for beaver) are excellent practice, but if they turn into nothing more than scoring, weaker pupils give up.

## How to use this tomorrow

1. **Draw a grid on the floor or the board** and play "The robot teacher" in the last 15 minutes of a lesson. You need nothing but chalk and paper.
2. **Introduce the phrase "Let's find the bug"** instead of "That's wrong". Finding the mistake in an instruction is a skill, not a punishment.
3. **Print the binary cards** from the CS Unplugged website (there is a BiH version too) and use them in a maths lesson in grade 3 or 4.
4. **Look at old Bebras tasks** for the younger age groups and pick one a week as a "brain teaser" to start a lesson.
5. **Suggest a game for parents at home:** the child tells a parent how to make a sandwich, and the parent follows the instructions literally. Lots of laughter, and a clear lesson: instructions have to be exact.
