Everyone has an opinion about artificial intelligence (AI) in the classroom, but hardly anyone has data. That changed a little this week: on 10 November 2026, researchers at Stanford University published a review of every rigorous causal study of generative AI in education they could find. The conclusion is neither "ban it" nor "roll it out everywhere", but something more practical: what matters most is what the tool is used for.

## What exactly was published

The review comes from the SCALE Initiative, a research centre at Stanford, and updates a report published in March this year. In March, 20 studies met the criteria; now there are 52. Of the 868 papers published between October 2025 and June 2026 that the team added to its database, only 32 passed the quality check.

Why so few? The authors count only studies that can show cause and effect: experiments in which participants are randomly split into those who use the tool and those who don't, plus a few quasi-experimental studies. Surveys in which students say AI helps them do not count.

Of the 52 studies, 42 look at tools used by students and 12 at tools for teachers (a few cover both).

## Finding 1: it helps when it explains, and harms when it does the work

The most important distinction in the review is between two kinds of use. The first is AI as support for learning: the student asks for an explanation, practises with feedback or checks their own work. The second is AI as a substitute for work: the tool writes the essay, solves the problem or prepares the presentation.

For the second kind, the data are fairly clear. In one study, 85 Brazilian university students prepared a presentation. Those who used ChatGPT spent on average 3.2 hours instead of 5.8, but on an unannounced comprehension test 45 days later, the students who had worked without it did better. An analysis of 3.2 million answers on an American maths practice platform (from 5th grade to university) showed that after ChatGPT appeared, students spent less time on word problems, which are easy to paste into a chatbot. On supervised tests, their chance of solving such a problem correctly fell by about 25 percent compared with problems built around graphs, which are harder to paste.

For the first kind, the picture is more favourable, but not consistent. Some studies find gains, others find no difference compared with a textbook or an ordinary practice platform. The authors say so openly: even use "for learning" does not reliably improve results.

One finding undercuts the common assumption that "educational" AI tools are better by design than an ordinary chatbot. Among 241 German students in grades 7 to 9, a chatbot with additional pedagogical instructions produced no more learning than plain ChatGPT.

## Finding 2: students like it, but that doesn't mean they learned

Students in these studies often rate working with AI as easier, more interesting and more useful. That impression often does not match their test results.

An example: 391 German students in grades 10 and 11 received feedback on a draft essay in English, some from teachers and others from the GPT-4 model. Students rated the instant AI feedback as more useful and motivating, but the quality of the revised essays and of later writing did not differ significantly between the groups. For teachers this is partly good news, because the AI feedback was no worse than theirs. It is also a warning that student satisfaction is not a measure of learning.

## Finding 3: AI in teachers' hands saves time, but at a cost

An earlier study in England (259 teachers in 68 schools) showed that ChatGPT cut lesson preparation by about 25 minutes a week, with no measurable drop in the quality of the plans. Newer studies show that university teaching assistants, when given AI suggestions, give students more feedback without spending more time.

A new study from Turkey calls for caution. There, 193 teachers (with a total of 2,816 students in grades 5 to 12) were given a chatbot to support their teaching for one semester. Average exam grades did not change, but students' motivation and self-confidence declined, even though students did not know their teachers were using AI. Among students of weaker teachers, grades fell as well. Teachers mostly used the tool to produce materials, which they adopted with minimal changes. The authors interpret this as handing teaching work over to the tool, while noting that the study does not prove this was the cause.

## An example from practice: maths lessons in Sierra Leone

The strongest positive result in the review comes from Sierra Leone. For eight weeks, teachers brought Google's Gemini Guided Learning tool into half of the maths lessons for 1,423 students in grades 7 and 8. How they did it matters: students worked in pairs, the teacher set the goal of the lesson, the instructions for the tool were prepared in advance, and every lesson ended with a whole-class discussion. Maths scores rose by 0.258 standard deviations, with a larger effect among students who were stronger in maths at the start.

Compare this with an American study of 6,997 students in grades 6 to 8 at 20 schools. There, an AI tutor offering hints and explanations was added to an existing practice platform. Students practised more slowly and solved fewer problems, and a delayed knowledge test showed no overall gain.

The two studies cannot be compared directly, but both fit the authors' conclusion that the way a tool is introduced may matter more than the tool itself. The OECD made a similar point on 8 September, when it published the results of PISA 2025, the international assessment taken by more than 760,000 fifteen-year-olds in 91 countries and economies: digital and AI tools should be used carefully, for clearly defined purposes and in moderation.

## Limitations

- Most papers in the review are arXiv preprints that have not yet been peer-reviewed.
- Most of the new studies were carried out with university students, not schoolchildren. Only five new studies of student-facing tools were conducted in American primary and secondary schools, and the authors warn against generalising the findings.
- None of the samples described comes from Bosnia and Herzegovina or the neighbouring Western Balkan countries. The closest data come from Turkey.
- Where gains were measured, they were mostly checked a few days to a week later. There are no data yet on long-term effects.
- The review says almost nothing about equity: which students AI helps and which it holds back.
- Some older studies used models such as GPT-3.5, which have since been replaced by newer ones.

## How to use this tomorrow

1. **Separate "AI for learning" from "AI for doing".** Tell students exactly what is allowed: asking for an explanation, an example, self-check questions or a comment on their draft. A finished essay or a solved problem is not, and the figures in this article explain why.
2. **Measure learning without the tool.** A few days after a lesson in which you used AI, give a short check on paper. Students enjoying the lesson is not proof that they learned.
3. **Build AI into the structure of the lesson, not in place of it.** The Sierra Leone model is easy to adopt: pair work, a clear lesson goal, instructions prepared in advance and a whole-class discussion at the end.
4. **Always rework the materials AI makes for you.** Use the tool for narrow tasks (quiz questions, a draft of feedback) and adapt the result to your class before it reaches students.
5. **Parents: ask your child to explain their homework to you in their own words, away from the screen.** If they can't, the tool did the homework.
