We are used to worrying about teenagers who don't read. The latest data from the United Kingdom suggest the problem is moving further down: children aged 5 to 8 are the only group whose reading enjoyment fell again this year. That is the age of the first three grades of primary school, exactly when the reading habit is still being built.

## What was studied

The National Literacy Trust, a UK literacy charity, runs the Annual Literacy Survey every year, asking how much children read and whether they enjoy it. The 2026 survey ran from January to March, and the report was published in June. It included 125,375 children and young people aged 5 to 18 from 479 schools across the United Kingdom, the largest sample in the survey's history.

It is important to understand what is being measured: this is not a reading test, but a questionnaire about attitudes and habits. Children themselves say whether they like reading in their free time and how often they do it.

## Main findings

**A modest recovery among older children.** Among 8 to 18-year-olds, the share who say they enjoy reading rose from 32.7% in 2025 to 36.1% in 2026. It is the first increase after several years of decline, but still far below the level of about ten years ago, when the share was above half. About one in five children (20.3%) read daily.

**A decline among the youngest, for the third year in a row.** Among 5 to 8-year-olds, reading enjoyment fell from 62.6% to 61.6%. The number still looks respectable, but the trend is worrying: according to figures cited by ReadingWise, the share was over 76% in 2019. 45.5% of children this age read daily, compared with 56% in 2020.

**Boys are falling further behind.** The decline among the youngest is driven by boys, while enjoyment among girls rose slightly. The gap between them at this age widened from about 7.6 to 10.8 percentage points.

**Poorer children are recovering more slowly.** Among children receiving free school meals (in the UK an indicator of lower family income), reading enjoyment rose much less than among their peers, so the gap more than doubled in a single year.

**Encouragement and interests matter.** Two thirds of children (65.8%) say their teachers encourage them to read, and just over half (54.8%) say their parents do. Almost half (48.7%) say reading helps them explore what they are interested in, and this reason is especially important for the children who otherwise read the least.

## Why this matters in Bosnia and Herzegovina too

The UK data say nothing directly about our children, but they fit a wider picture. The international PIRLS study, which measures reading among fourth-graders, found in its 2021 cycle that on average 42% of pupils "very much like" reading and 18% do not. Pupils who like reading scored 513 points on average, those who do not 491. The report's authors describe this relationship as moderate. It does not follow that a love of reading raises scores on its own, since attitudes and achievement influence each other, but it is clear that they go together.

The new cycle, PIRLS 2026, has just finished collecting data, and results are expected in December 2027. The final list of 63 education systems on the IEA website includes Serbia, Montenegro, North Macedonia and Albania, but not Bosnia and Herzegovina. This means we will get new data on the reading attitudes of ten-year-olds in the region, while for BiH we will still have to rely on other countries' experience and our own observations.

## A practical example: a library children choose

In the same report, the National Literacy Trust presents results from its primary school programmes, such as Libraries for Primaries (equipping and reviving school libraries) and the Young Readers Programme (events where children choose books to take home themselves). Among children who were least interested in reading at the start, 77% showed progress in reading enjoyment, 84% in confidence and 66% in the habit of reading daily.

What these programmes have in common is not more compulsory reading, but choice, access to books and adults who show that reading is a normal part of the day. That can be transferred to any of our schools, even without a big budget.

## Limitations

- **These are self-reports.** Children aged 5 to 8 answer simplified questions, and their answers depend on how they understand them and what kind of day they had. A one-percentage-point change in a single year is not a strong signal on its own; the multi-year trend matters more.
- **The UK context is not ours.** There, 2026 was declared the National Year of Reading, with a campaign involving thousands of libraries and hundreds of partners. Part of the increase among older children may be due to that very campaign, which would not automatically be repeated here.
- **The programme results were published by the organisation that runs them.** The figures on children's progress are useful as guidance, but they are not an independent evaluation with a control group.
- **Enjoyment is not the same as skill.** A child can love picture books and still struggle with decoding. Work on reading technique and work on motivation go hand in hand; one does not replace the other.

## How to start tomorrow

1. **Introduce ten minutes of free, ungraded reading (grades 1–5).** The child chooses what to read, including comics, children's encyclopedias or magazines. No comprehension questions, no notes; the goal is for reading not always to be tied to a task. In grades 1 and 2 this can be "reading" pictures and browsing a book together in pairs.
2. **Build a classroom shelf based on interests.** Ask pupils what they are interested in (football, animals, space, video games) and collect a few books on those topics from the school or town library or from parents. The data show that interests are the best way in for the children who read the least, and these are often boys.
3. **Keep reading aloud in grades 4 and 5.** Reading aloud usually stops as soon as children master decoding, which is exactly when many lose their connection to stories. A few minutes of an exciting book at the end of a lesson keeps curiosity alive.
4. **Give parents one concrete message.** Instead of "read more", suggest: let your child see you reading too, let them choose a library book on their own, and talk about the book the way you would about a film, without quizzing. According to the survey, parental encouragement helps children read even when reading is not their favourite activity.
5. **In subject teaching (grades 6–9), link reading to your subject.** A biology, history or computer science teacher can recommend one popular book or article from their field. That way reading stops being just a language-class assignment and becomes part of what children are interested in.
