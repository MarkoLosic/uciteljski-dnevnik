## Introduction

The difference between a useless and a useful answer from an AI tool usually isn't the tool, it's the request. "Make me a maths lesson" produces generic text that fits neither the year group nor the children. A request that says who the pupils are, what they should learn and what format you need gives you something you can use straight away.

Here are eight prompts for the most common tasks in primary teaching. Copy them, replace the parts in square brackets and adapt them to your class. All of them are written so that they **need no information about specific children** (more on that in [What you should never type into ChatGPT about your pupils](/en/blog/sta-ne-upisivati-u-chatgpt-o-ucenicima)).

## What a good prompt looks like

A good request has five parts:

1. **Role.** Who you're addressing: "You are an experienced primary teacher."
2. **Context.** Year group, subject, topic, number of pupils, time available.
3. **Goal.** What the children should know or be able to do after the lesson.
4. **Constraints.** Language, length, level of difficulty, materials you have.
5. **Format.** Table, list, printable text, number of examples.

If you leave any of these out, the AI fills the gaps by guessing.

## 1. Lesson plan

```text
You are an experienced primary teacher. Create a 45-minute lesson plan for
[year group], subject [subject], topic "[topic]".
Goal: by the end of the lesson pupils should [specific outcome].
Split the lesson into a starter (5 min), main part (30 min) and plenary
(10 min). Include at least one activity with movement or pair work in the
main part. List the materials needed. Use British English.
```

## 2. Tasks at three levels of difficulty

```text
Create five tasks on "[area]" for [year group] at three levels: core,
developing and challenge. Tasks at the same level should vary in form
(calculation, word problem, fill in the gap). Put the answers separately
at the end so I can cut them off before printing.
```

## 3. A short reading text

```text
Write a short story of about [number] words for [year group] pupils who
are just learning to read. Use short sentences and familiar words.
Topic: [topic]. The main character is [an animal or object]. Don't use the
names of real people. Add three comprehension questions at the end.
```

## 4. Questions for discussing a text

```text
For the story "[title]", write discussion questions for [year group]
pupils in three groups: questions about facts in the text, questions about
how the characters feel, and "what would you do" questions. Four per group.
```

## 5. A game for revision

```text
Suggest three games for revising "[topic]" with [year group]. Each game
should last 10 minutes, involve the whole class and need no materials
beyond a board, chalk and paper. For each game, write the rules in five
steps and how to adapt it for pupils who need more support.
```

## 6. A note to parents

```text
Write a short, friendly note to parents of [year group] pupils about
[event: school trip, parents' evening, project]. Include the date, time and
what the children need to bring. Tone: warm and clear, no jargon.
Maximum 120 words.
```

## 7. Written feedback

```text
Write five examples of written feedback on [year group] pupils' work on
"[task]". Each example should include: what was done well, what needs
improving and one specific next step. The tone is supportive. Don't invent
marks or grades.
```

These are example phrasings, not finished feedback. You write the actual feedback for each child yourself, based on what you've seen.

## 8. Let the AI check itself

This prompt comes straight from software testing: a result isn't accepted until someone checks it. After any answer, send:

```text
Review what you've written as a strict reviewer. List: possible factual
errors, tasks without a single clear answer, words that are too difficult
for [year group] and places where you didn't follow my constraints.
```

Don't trust this answer blindly either, but it often catches things you would have missed.

## Check before you print

AI tools write convincingly even when they're wrong. Before anything reaches the children:

- **Do all the tasks yourself.** Calculation errors and tasks with no solution are not rare.
- **Check the facts.** Dates, names, natural phenomena, geography.
- **Check the language.** AI easily mixes British and American spelling and can slip in words that are too advanced.
- **Read it through a child's eyes.** Would a six-year-old understand every word?
- **Match it to your plan.** Does the task really lead to the outcome in your curriculum?

## Conclusion

Prompts are not magic formulas. They are well-asked questions. The more clearly you say what you need, the less time you spend fixing the result.

You get the best results when you treat AI as an assistant that writes the first draft, while you remain the one who knows the children, checks the work and makes the decisions.
