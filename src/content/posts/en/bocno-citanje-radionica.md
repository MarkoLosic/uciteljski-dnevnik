At school we teach students to read a text carefully, from beginning to end. Online, that habit works against them: the longer they stay on a page of doubtful origin, the more convincing it seems. This one-lesson workshop practises the opposite move, the one professional fact-checkers use.

![A teacher sits at a computer surrounded by open browser windows: search results, Wikipedia pages and magnifying glasses over unfamiliar websites, with check marks for sources that have been verified](/slike/bocno-citanje-radionica.jpg)

## Why lateral reading

Between June 2018 and May 2019, Stanford researchers tested 3,446 American high school students, who had free access to the internet while completing the tasks. The results were poor: 52 percent of students accepted an anonymous video, actually filmed in Russia, as "strong evidence" of voter fraud in the US; two thirds could not tell news from advertisements labelled as sponsored content; and 96 percent did not consider that a climate website's ties to the fossil fuel industry reduced its credibility. Students relied on the look of the page, the domain and what the site said about itself.

The same group had earlier found that both historians and university students were more easily fooled by dubious websites than professional fact-checkers. The difference came down to one move: fact-checkers read "laterally". They don't linger on an unfamiliar page; they open new tabs and look for what others say about the source. Mike Caulfield summed up the same approach in the SIFT method, four moves: stop, investigate the source, find better coverage, and trace claims and quotes back to their original context.

Importantly, this can be learned. In a study published in 2022 in the *Journal of Educational Psychology*, about 500 high school students from one urban school district received six 50-minute lessons. Afterwards, they were twice as successful at spotting dubious websites as they had been before the lessons.

There are similar workshops in Bosnia and Herzegovina, usually outside regular lessons. As part of the "Youth for Better Media" project, they were held in 2023 in Zavidovići, Bijeljina, Brčko, Jajce and Travnik. The workshop below is designed so that a teacher can run it alone, in a regular lesson.

## Workshop: "Who is behind this?"

**Goal:** before reading an unfamiliar source, the student leaves the page and within a few minutes finds out who is behind it and what others say about it.

**Age:** 13 to 18 (variations for younger students are at the end).

**Duration:** 45 minutes.

**Materials:** a projector and a computer with internet access; one phone or computer per group of three to four students; a printed worksheet for each group; a board.

**Preparation (20 minutes):** choose three sources on a health, nutrition or science topic and check them yourself in advance.

- **Source A:** the website of an institution whose reliability is easy to check (e.g. a public health institute).
- **Source B:** an organisation whose interest is not visible at first glance. Stanford researchers cite the International Life Sciences Institute: on its website it presents itself as a non-profit devoted to health science, while a quick search shows that it is backed by large food companies.
- **Source C:** a post or screenshot circulating on social media with no source given.

### Step by step

**1. First vote (5 minutes).** Show source B on the projector and slowly go through the home page and the "About us" section. Ask: "How much would you trust this site, from 1 to 5?" Write the result of the vote on the board and don't comment on it.

**2. Demonstration (7 minutes).** Say: "Now I'm going to do what fact-checkers do. I won't read another word on this page." Open a new tab, type in the organisation's name and go through the results out loud: who writes about it, what Wikipedia says, who funds it. Then write three questions on the board:

- Who is behind this source?
- What do other, independent sources say about it?
- Can I find the same claim somewhere more reliable?

**3. Group work (15 minutes).** Each group gets sources A and C and a worksheet. The rule: for the first two minutes nobody reads the source itself, only new tabs are allowed. Groups have at most seven minutes per source. Walk around and ask only one thing: "How do you know that?"

**4. Reports (8 minutes).** In one minute, each group says which source it trusts more and which search showed them that. Note useful moves on the board (searching the name, checking Wikipedia, looking for the same story in other media, reverse image search).

**5. Second vote and discussion (7 minutes).** Repeat the vote on source B and compare it with the first one. Lead the discussion using the questions below.

**6. Exit ticket (3 minutes).** On a slip of paper, each student completes the sentence: "Next time I come across an unfamiliar source, I will first…"

### Worksheet (one column per source)

| Question | Source A | Source C |
|---|---|---|
| Who is behind the source? What did we type into the search? | | |
| What do two other sources say about it? | | |
| Does someone more reliable publish the same claim? Who? | | |
| Our reliability rating (1–5) and one reason | | |

### Discussion questions

- In the first vote, what made you trust or distrust the site?
- What did you learn off the page that you would never have found on it?
- Why is the "About us" section not proof of reliability?
- What do we do when we find nothing about a source?
- In everyday life, when is it worth taking two minutes to check, and when isn't it?

### Variations for different ages

- **Ages 10 to 12:** no devices for students. You do the search on the projector while the class "tells" you what to type. Instead of the worksheet, use a single question: "Who is telling us this, and how do we know?"
- **Ages 13 to 15:** the workshop as described, with topics close to students (nutrition, sport, video games).
- **Ages 16 to 18:** add a fourth task, tracing a quote or photo back to where it was first published, and ask the group to find a better source for the same claim.
- **No internet in the classroom:** print screenshots of the search results for each source and hand them out in envelopes as a "case file".

## Limitations

One lesson is not enough. In the study mentioned above, students had six lessons, and even after that they scored on average only half of the possible points. The research was carried out in a single American school district and funded by Google.org, so the results should not be transferred directly to our classrooms.

Lateral reading depends on what can be found about a source. There is often almost nothing about small local news portals, and Wikipedia articles in our languages are shorter and fewer than in English. The absence of a trail is information, but it is not proof that a source is bad.

There are also two practical risks. The first is that students conclude that nobody can be trusted; that is why the workshop always includes a reliable source and ends with the question of where to find a better one. The second is the choice of examples: current political topics easily turn the lesson into a debate about opinions, so it is safer to start with health, nutrition and science.

## How to use this tomorrow

1. Choose one source from your subject and check it laterally yourself; time how long it took you.
2. Write the three questions from the board on paper and stick them up in the classroom, next to the projector.
3. Whenever you use a website in class, take two minutes to show students who is behind it.
4. In research homework, ask for one sentence with every source: "I trust this source because…"
5. Parents can do the same at home: when a child shows you an interesting video or post, open a new tab together and look up who published it.
