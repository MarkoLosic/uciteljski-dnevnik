In early September, the PISA 2025 results were published for 91 countries and economies. Bosnia and Herzegovina is not among them, for the second cycle in a row, so the most recent internationally comparable data on our 15-year-olds is eight years old. This is not a story about a place in the rankings. It is about the fact that a system that doesn't measure cannot know whether what it does is working.

![An empty classroom: an hourglass, a sheet of paper and a pencil on a desk, and on the wall a map of Bosnia and Herzegovina made of puzzle pieces showing books and charts, with some pieces missing or lying on the floor](/slike/bih-bez-pisa-rezultata.jpg)

## What exactly happened

BiH took part in PISA only once, in 2018, alongside 78 other countries. The results were sobering: 58 percent of students did not reach the minimum level of proficiency in mathematics, 54 percent in reading and 57 percent in science.

After that, two cycles were missed, 2022 and 2025. According to statements by the Agency for Pre-school, Primary and Secondary Education (APOSO) reported in the media, the obstacle was the same both times: the Presidency of BiH did not give its consent to sign the agreement with the OECD. Behind this lies a dispute over who is responsible for education and how the results would be presented.

The next opportunity is PISA 2029. The BiH Ministry of Civil Affairs and APOSO discussed it as early as July 2025, and the Agency says preparatory activities are included in its 2026 work programme. If that happens, eleven years will pass between two measurements, which is an entire school career, from first grade to almost the end of secondary school.

## Why this is a systemic problem

In 2022, the OECD and UNICEF published a review of evaluation and assessment in education in BiH. Two findings from that review explain why a missed test is more than a missed headline.

First, governance is split across fourteen administrative units and four levels of government. Education policy is run by Republika Srpska, ten cantons in the Federation of BiH and the Brčko District, each on its own. Second, the review notes that BiH has little comparable data on teaching and learning, and that it is precisely the limited cooperation at state level that prevents regular participation in international studies such as PISA.

The consequence is practical. Several education systems in the country are introducing outcome-based curricula and changing textbooks and assessment rules. Without measurement before and after, nobody can reliably say whether these changes helped students, harmed them or changed nothing at all.

A comparison with our neighbours shows the difference. Serbia's results fell in all three areas in the 2025 cycle: according to the published results, from 447 to 429 points in science, from 440 to 415 in reading and from 440 to 417 in mathematics. That is bad news, but it is news you can work with: we know how big the drop is, in which areas and since when. In BiH, the same question can only be answered with guesswork.

## What we do have

The picture is not completely blank, and that is worth knowing.

**TIMSS for fourth grade.** BiH took part in the 2019 and 2023 cycles. Both times, achievement was below the TIMSS scale centrepoint of 500; in mathematics in 2023 the average was 447 points. Along with the results, APOSO also published recommendations for BiH. This is the only trend we currently have, but it concerns ten-year-olds, not students at the end of compulsory schooling.

**ICILS 2023.** BiH also took part in the International Computer and Information Literacy Study, which was likewise carried out by the Agency.

**External exams within individual systems.** Sarajevo Canton has held an external exam at the end of primary school since the 2012/2013 school year. Tuzla Canton has a standardised external assessment at the end of primary school. In Republika Srpska, external assessment of ninth-grade students has been carried out since 2013, and a final primary school exam was introduced later.

The Sarajevo Canton example shows both the reach and the limits of such a solution. Every year the canton gets data on all of its ninth-graders, by school and by subject, which is more than most systems in the country have. But a test from Sarajevo cannot be compared with a test from Tuzla or Banja Luka, because the tasks, curricula and scoring are different. Adding up local exams does not give a picture of the country.

## Limitations

It would be dishonest to present PISA as the solution.

- **PISA measures narrowly.** It tests 15-year-olds in three areas, on a sample, once every few years. It says nothing about an individual school, about the school's role in upbringing or about subjects outside those areas.
- **Taking part does not, by itself, raise achievement.** Serbia participates regularly and its results are falling. Data is the start of the work, not the work itself.
- **Results are easily misused.** Rankings attract attention, but the useful information is in the details: differences between schools, the influence of family background, the types of tasks students get wrong.
- **We have data we don't use.** In public discussions after the PISA 2025 results were published, experts warned that little is done in BiH even with the findings of studies that already exist. A new cycle won't help if it meets the same fate.
- **High-stakes external exams come at a cost.** When points decide admission to secondary school, teaching in the final grade easily slides into drilling task types.

## What you can do tomorrow

The decision about PISA 2029 is not in the hands of teachers or parents. But the lack of data at system level can be partly made up for at school level.

1. **Agree on one common test per grade within your subject team.** Same tasks, same scoring criteria, all classes, twice a year. The goal is not to compare teachers, but for the team to see which learning outcomes students haven't mastered, regardless of who teaches them.
2. **Use publicly released PISA and TIMSS items.** One such item a week, as an application task, shows students and the teacher the difference between repeating material and using it.
3. **Keep results from year to year.** A simple table with the percentage of correct answers per learning outcome is enough for a school to have its own trend after two or three years, which the state currently doesn't have.
4. **Parents: ask for more than the grade average.** Where an external exam exists, ask the school how students did by subject and what the school plans to do with those findings. At home, ask your child to explain where they could use what they learned, instead of just repeating the lesson.
5. **Principals and school counsellors: read the TIMSS 2023 recommendations for BiH.** It is the most recent comparable finding we have, and it concerns primary school, where falling behind is easiest to prevent.
