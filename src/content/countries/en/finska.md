## What Primary Teaching Looks Like

Finnish children start school at age seven — a year later than in Bosnia and Herzegovina — after one year of compulsory pre-primary education. Through the end of grade 9, all children attend the same comprehensive school without academic tracking.

During the first six grades, a single classroom teacher typically teaches most or all subjects, much like classroom teaching in Southeastern Europe except lasting one year longer. The Finnish National Agency for Education sets the national core curriculum, while municipalities and schools write their own local curricula from it.

## The Teacher as an Autonomous Professional

The decisive difference happens before anyone steps into a classroom. Every classroom teacher in Finland holds a master's degree, university admission is selective, and the profession carries genuine social respect. That is why the system relies on professional trust rather than external policing: there is no school inspectorate, and quality is tracked through self-evaluation and sample-based assessments.

## Unhurried Assessment

In grades 1–3, children mostly receive descriptive feedback explaining what they can already do, what they are practicing, and where they need help. Numerical grades on a 4–10 scale are introduced by grade 4 at the latest. There are no mandatory national tests for whole cohorts.

Headlines often claim that Finland “abolished school subjects.” It did not: the 2014 core curriculum requires at least one multidisciplinary learning module per year — widely known as phenomenon-based learning — alongside regular school subjects, not instead of them.

## Twenty Years of Decline

In 2006, Finland scored 546 in PISA reading. By PISA 2025, it stood at 474 in reading and 469 in mathematics — down 16 and 15 points since 2022 alone — placing around 17th in reading. Notably, scores among students from more affluent families fell fastest in the latest cycle. Finland also dropped 17 points in grade 4 reading in PIRLS 2021. While it still scores above the OECD average in all three PISA subjects, the downward trend is unmistakable, and the country has very little national cohort data to diagnose classroom-level gaps.

## How Finland Is Responding

- Since August 1, 2025, national law has banned phones and other mobile devices during lessons unless a teacher authorizes them for learning or health reasons.
- A major reform of learning support took effect in August 2025 across pre-primary and comprehensive education.
- The government is investing 200 million euros to add weekly instructional hours in early reading, writing, and arithmetic.

## What We Could Borrow

- **Descriptive feedback as real guidance in the early grades**, not as paperwork alongside a numerical grade.
- **Rigorous teacher preparation and professional standing** as the precondition for wider classroom autonomy.
- **Not flying blind without data.** Finland's two-decade slide is a reminder that trust in teachers does not replace regular system-wide measurement of student learning.
