## Why Estonia Stands Out

Estonia is a small country working with a modest budget, yet in PISA 2025 its 15-year-olds once again ranked first in Europe in mathematics and science and second in reading, just a single point behind Ireland. That makes it a more practical reference point for Southeastern Europe than Finland: it shows that a strong school system does not depend on national wealth.

## The Early Grades

Children start school at age seven. Basic school lasts nine years, divided into three three-year stages. In the first stage, a classroom teacher teaches most subjects, and the law caps classes at 24 students. Municipalities are required to provide a school close to home at least through grade 6.

Each school chooses how to assess students during the first six grades. It may rely on descriptive feedback without numbers, provided those assessments are translated onto the standard 1–5 scale by the end of grade 6.

## Equal Opportunity in Practice

The most telling number about Estonia is not its average score, but its gap. In science, the score difference between the most advantaged quarter of students and the most disadvantaged quarter is 68 points, compared with an OECD average of 86. The OECD places Estonia among just seven education systems worldwide that combine high performance, wide inclusion, and a weak link between family background and achievement. There is no academic tracking before the end of grade 9.

## Transition to Estonian-Language Instruction

Starting in 2024, all children entering grade 1 began studying in Estonian for the first time. The rollout advances one cohort at a time, covering grades 1–6 in 2026/27 and reaching completion by 2030. The state has set Estonian-proficiency requirements for teachers and provides targeted transition support to schools.

## Digital Tools Without the Hype

Electronic gradebooks and digital exams have been routine for years. In 2026, the national “AI Leap” (TI-Hüpe) initiative is expanding to another 38,000 students and 2,000 teachers in grade 10 and vocational schools.

## Current Pressures

Estonia is not immune to decline. Its PISA 2025 reading score is its lowest since joining PISA — down 12 points from 2022 and 24 points from 2018. The share of low-performing readers is rising, teacher shortages are growing, and the teaching workforce is aging. Moving rapidly to Estonian-language instruction also carries real risks for children from Russian-speaking families.

## What We Could Borrow

- **One common school for every child through the end of primary education**, monitoring gaps between schools rather than national averages alone.
- **A clear legal cap on class size**, with any exceptions requiring formal justification.
- **School autonomy to use descriptive grading in the early grades**, paired with clear rules on how it converts to numbers later on.
- **National exit exams at the end of grade 9** as a shared benchmark across the whole country.
